This post introduces what I'm working on right now, Policy Language Models. The paper is linked at the end.

*Updated August 2026, with the definitions stated in full, the experimental program, and its results.*

## The trajectory

Frontier large language models and the harnesses built around them can now tackle complex long-horizon problems, resolve software issues, conduct deep research, and drive hours-long sessions. These gains have largely come from inference-time scaling. Models are trained to spend more tokens deliberating, and ReAct systems extend that capability by interleaving reasoning with actions and observations. ReAct remains the dominant substrate for this work. We define a ReAct trajectory as a growing sequence of thoughts, actions, and observations.

In ReAct, the trajectory only grows, and LLM outputs degrade as the context window fills. The most popular general approach to solve this is context condensation or compaction, where context from user requests or agent trajectories is repeatedly summarized when it exceeds a length threshold. Unfortunately, context compaction loses information and therefore is not a good solution to this problem. Trajectories pushed to length fail in characteristic ways, exploration that fragments the reasoning, self-reflection that strains against load-bearing mistakes, the capacity of the window itself; the short version is a single mismatch. The model repeatedly reaches for reasoning shapes the autoregressive substrate cannot hold: it branches to weigh alternatives, reverts to undo a wrong step, and explores before committing, yet every such move is flattened back into one forward sequence. We believe these failures act as a dampener on reasoning LLMs, and that solving them will yield far more capable reasoners.

The failures are not only about length. A ReAct loop bundles two separable things, the capability of interleaving thought with code, and the protocol that ends the call: a disclosed round budget, an obligation to answer, a return channel, pressure at the end. Frameworks ship them fused, because a call that cannot end is useless to a caller who only receives a value. In my own runs the protocol failed on its own terms, sub-agents surrendering early and returning nothing, returning a progress report where an answer belonged, returning the literal string `done`. These are artifacts of the channel, not of the deduction, and they appear wherever the channel is present.

## Taking work off the line

Recently, [Zhang, Kraska, and Khattab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24601) introduced Recursive Language Models (RLMs), a general-purpose inference paradigm that dramatically scales the effective input and output lengths of LLMs. Their key insight is that an arbitrarily long prompt should not be fed directly into the neural network but should be treated as part of the environment the LLM interacts with, symbolically and recursively. An RLM exposes the same external interface as an LLM: a string prompt in and a string response out. Given a prompt $$P$$, it initializes a REPL environment in which $$P$$ is set as the value of a variable, and the model writes code that peeks into and decomposes $$P$$, observes the effects of execution, and invokes the LLM itself on as many slices of the input as necessary. By treating the prompt itself as an external object and enabling symbolic recursion, RLMs tackle limitations of expressive power in recent work on coding agents, retrieval agents, and sub-agent delegation. What leaves the trajectory, however, is only the input/output processing. Thinking, exploration, reflection, action, and observation still accumulate on the root trajectory, and the thinking is still done the ReAct way.

I first read this as a result about input processing, and the paragraph above still frames it that way. Zhang's [work over the summer](https://alexzhang13.github.io/blog/2026/harness/) makes the actual claim explicit, and it is about composition. A harness induces an equivalence relation over tasks: two problems that share latent structure, however different their vocabulary and their length, reduce to nearly the same token trajectory in the root, because the harness offloads the domain-specific material into sub-calls and exposes only the structure needed to choose the next operation. A good harness shapes each call so that every observation is, in his phrase, locally in-distribution. What he demonstrates is that training on short tasks generalizes to tasks eight to thirty-two times longer, and that training on one domain transfers to others. Input length is where this is measured. It is not what is being claimed.

This matters here because it is the strongest available statement of what a harness is for, and it is not the statement I was making. His invariant is a property of the model's input distribution, that every call the model sees looks like something it was trained on. Ours is a property of the trajectory's information state. Those are different objects, and most of this post is about the second one. He hedges the reach of his own conjecture, that the tasks people care about can "apparently almost always" be decomposed naturally, and he offers no characterization of the training set that would suffice; I read the hedges as honest rather than as an opening.

## Policy Language Models

We introduce Policy Language Models (PLMs), a general-purpose meta-reasoning inference paradigm for scaling the reasoning power of LLMs. The key insights are that reasoning and expressing the computation for a task cost far fewer tokens than the LLM doing the task itself, and that the ability to express different computational shapes leaves room for neuro-symbolic programs authored by the PLM that do the task better than an LLM doing it autoregressively. Meta-reasoning is the reasoning behavior built on these two facts: reasoning intertwined with the computations it authors. This behavior sheds the failures plaguing ReAct while keeping all its benefits, offers additional benefits, and, we believe, carries a far higher ceiling for reasoning LLMs.

Call the main trajectory sufficient if no information added to it from the work done off it would significantly increase the information available to the LLM, and minimal if it is sufficient in the fewest tokens possible. We formally define meta-reasoning as the behavior that keeps the main trajectory sufficient and near-minimal by creating and delegating to computations and assimilating their work. A PLM has two parts: the harness, where computations are authored and run as policies in a persistent REPL environment, and the model, trained to achieve the meta-reasoning behavior.

What an RLM does for data processing, a PLM does for all the work that a trajectory would otherwise hold. Since data processing is a type of computation, RLM capabilities follow as a special case. The computations a PLM generates are called policies. These policies can be run, edited, and composed dynamically, which makes it easier to express and carry out the trajectory's reasoning and work through them. The harness provides a small set of LLM primitives, which are the elementary capabilities of a reasoning LLM, enabling the PLM to create neuro-symbolic programs. An LLM policy is any policy that contains these primitives. Such a policy is programmed rather than called as a tool. By contrast, an RLM's sub-call takes a query string under a system prompt fixed by its harness. This core distinction makes LLM policies more expressive than an RLM's programs. Although delegation is easier for the trajectory because tool calls carry no reasoning overhead, building policies provides greater expressive power. Because the neural and symbolic parts are deeply integrated in our harness, this differs from RLMs, where it is akin to a tool call. As a result, a PLM can build a far greater variety of LLM policies than simple tool-call delegation allows.

Meta-reasoning requires the main trajectory to have control over all the work the harness performs. An RLM's root trajectory lacks this control, by design and by intent. By design, a recursive sub-call is another RLM, so the root cannot vary what it may do beyond a certain extent, and the root does not have explicit control over its capabilities, including whether the sub-call delegates further. Deeper recursion has already been observed to degrade performance and increase cost. By intent, Zhang hypothesizes that frontier models are "severely underutilized due to sub-optimal use of individual language model calls" and proposes training models to decompose tasks such that each sub-task is in-distribution for a sub-call, with each sub-call autonomous over its piece. A PLM retains the aforementioned control because of how it must author policies: besides standard Python, it has access only to the LLM primitives defined above. So every neuro-symbolic policy is a program made of code and these elementary LLM capabilities.

Zhang proposed training models to decompose. We pose a different question: can an LLM be trained to author the neuro-symbolic program that solves the task? Decomposition is one type of neuro-symbolic program among many. The difference is that a decomposer must estimate what is in-distribution for its sub-calls, while the PLM must estimate the behavior of each of its LLM primitives and build programs around that behavior. A policy succeeds when it behaves as the PLM estimated it would, its code and its LLM primitives alike. In the same spirit, what Zhang asks of decompositions, we ask of neuro-symbolic programs at large.

The trajectory itself is responsible for abstract reasoning. There the PLM is meant to reason over the goal and author the policies that set its plans in motion: policies to test its hypotheses, policies to process inputs, policies to write and output code. A PLM's job is not to run systems but to reason; it engineers its instruments with an understanding of the goal, sees the work they did, and reasons onward. This is the behavior we theorize and train toward. And since reading an input is itself computation, the RLM capability comes along as a special case, a long input can sit in the environment and be worked through with policies.

What the substrate buys is also compounding. A policy authored once can be rerun, revised, and composed into larger ones, so the work of reasoning accumulates as artifacts rather than as tokens. The trajectory that once stretched to hold every reasoning structure within a context window can now express those structures as policies. The substrate alone, however, does not produce the behavior. In our experiments, current LLMs placed on the PLM harness do not spontaneously meta-reason, they grind the work inline out of habit, or scatter one-off delegations that are at best half-cooked. The disposition has to be trained in. We take that as the finding that matters: meta-reasoning is a trainable surface of improvement that today's models have never been optimized for.

## The harness

A Policy Language Model is a large language model that meta-reasons: it reasons about the goal on its own trajectory and expresses the computation as policies in a persistent programming environment $$\mathcal{E}$$. The environment is a Python REPL whose state survives across rounds. Each round the model thinks abstractly, then acts through one code tool call that executes in $$\mathcal{E}$$ and updates its state. A PLM trajectory holds only three things: the abstract reasoning, the code, and the code's output as the tool result.

Meta-reasoning is the axis of inference along which the model reasons about the goal and reasons about generating the computations that will help attain it. It is three abilities held together: the ability to reason abstractly about the goal; the ability to reason and generate optimal compute structures for tasks; and the behavior that binds the two, in which the model reasons about what to do and how to do it, inlines or expresses the task as computation, benefits from its result, and reasons on.

A policy is any computation the PLM expresses rather than performs. The definition is behavioral, not syntactic, and the harness gives it support: the `@policy` decorator turns a function or a class into a named artifact that persists across rounds, holds global scope so it is used by name and never imported, and can be edited in place and duplicated. A policy is identified by its source code, and the source is what persists, what is read back, and what is revised. Authored once, it can be rerun, edited, and composed into larger policies.

The harness gives the PLM its LLM policies to build with. `llm` is a single call. `react_auto` is an LLM with the python tool, run in ReAct rounds under the termination protocol, which is to say a conventional sub-agent. `react` is the same loop with the protocol removed: it runs exactly the rounds it is given and stops, still open, and the name `RETURN` does not exist inside it. The split came out of the failures above. Termination becomes a decision the policy makes from outside, having read what the sub-computation actually did, rather than a decision the sub-model makes under deadline pressure, and `react` injects no text of its own, so every word in a stepped trajectory is either the author's or the model's.

Bare minimum on their own, they can be used to build arbitrarily complex information processing circuits. The building, however, is deliberate work: every sub-llm call begins empty. Every input, helper, prompt, any policy it will use, the `@policy` decorator and the LLM policies themselves, are handed to it. Nothing about a sub-llm is ambient, so a working one is constructed, not summoned. Fanning out is never a reflex but a decision the PLM has to make and build, so it decomposes only when it decides to. And this costs no expressive power: a PLM can build an RLM, or even an unbounded-depth PLM, as a policy inside its depth-two harness. Computation can never be fully controlled or predicted, but a harness decides who holds what control there is. Ours concentrates it in the PLM. An RLM scatters it across the tree.

Constraints and weaving complete the harness. A constraint is a way to define invariants over a computation's output, authored as a typed class, and it is compiled into the schema the call decodes under, so the model cannot emit a violating structure in the first place. An LLM's judgment so becomes a typed value in ordinary control flow: `react_auto("...", constraint=Bool)` yields the validated value, or `None` when the budget runs out without one, and policy code branches on the result. Weaving makes trajectories the same kind of object: messages pass by reference, so a `react` run over a list leaves its turns on that list, and the list can be handed onward as an object, to a second policy that inspects the reasoning, never as pasted text; the PLM's own trajectory is available the same way, as a live copy. The same holds for state: a sub-agent's namespace is a dictionary the caller owns, everything the sub-computation defined readable and callable after the call and between rounds, and granting is putting a key in it.

## The invariant, under pressure

Sufficiency and minimality, defined above, are the pair the rest of this post tests, and the pair is the point. A long-context model has sufficiency without minimality, one window holding everything and losing whatever eviction takes. An aggressive offloader has minimality without sufficiency, a short root over sub-computations whose reasoning dies at the return. Zhang's invariant, every observation locally in-distribution, says what the model must generalize over. It does not say what the root can still reach.

An RLM cannot express sufficiency, and the check is the contract, not a benchmark: `rlm_query` returns a string, and no channel returns the turns. A sub-call's trajectory is invisible to the root, which holds only what the sub-model chose to say, where a PLM policy holds the trajectory itself, every turn the sub-model wrote, its code and its answers. Hidden reasoning tokens cross the wire for neither system, my own harness strips that field, so the comparison is over what actually crosses. Zhang notes that his roots keep printing task-specific information back into the main context, and calls it undesirable for generalization; I read it as a system straining against exactly this missing channel.

Role authorship is the other half of the contract argument. Under the string contract the mapping from provenance to role is fixed by protocol, code output enters as tool or user material and an assistant turn can only be genuinely generated, while a PLM policy places any content under any role: a commitment the model appears to have made, an observation the environment appears to have produced, a system update arriving mid-history.

## What I set out to show, and what happened

The theory above has a companion claim, and it is the claim I spent the summer trying to establish. If a PLM can hold objects an RLM cannot, the space of policies it can author should be strictly larger in a way that shows up as capability. Write $$\Pi_X$$ for the interaction structures a program can control under contract $$X$$, and $$R(\pi)$$ for the tasks a policy $$\pi$$ gets right; a trained PLM covers the union of $$R(\pi)$$ over the policies $$\theta$$ can write. The bet was that this union outruns what any decomposition-shaped system reaches. That would have been a direct challenge to Zhang's conjecture.

I built three harnesses to test it. One is the full PLM harness. One is the same harness with exactly the beyond-string-contract surface removed, so a policy there is arbitrary code around string-in, string-out calls: the agentic region, and, with the round budget fixed, the system message withheld, and the namespace switch off, the RLM region. The third is a further restricted variant for a separate experiment. A test asserts the diff between them, so the boundary is materialized rather than promised.

The comparison did not go my way. On an interactive deduction task the best agentic policy scored 0.70 against the best PLM policy's 0.60 over ten development instances, one instance apart, and pooled over every run the ordering holds at 0.80 against 0.53, with run-to-run variance large enough that the PLM arm scored five of six and then two of five on overlapping instances. One more number empties both figures of meaning as a barrier test: an offline pass over the ten knowledge books, done before any policy ran, put the task's own ceiling at 0.616 under its action budget, because the books over-claim and the observations rule out the gold artifact in seven instances of ten. Both arms were pressed against noise in the data, not against either contract.

The emulation was harder to argue with. I flattened the PLM policy into its best string-contract form, everything it did but re-fed as one labeled transcript, and it reproduced the PLM maximum exactly on the iteration set, beat it on the full ten, and did it at sixty-four percent of that arm's token cost. Instrumenting both arms said why. Graded against a reference table, re-feeding read the books at ninety-one percent exact-rule fidelity, precisely what the live agentic session read them at, while the best PLM arm sat at 77.2, and thirty-six of its thirty-eight lost rules came from a single instance where fidelity fell to 5.3 percent, a sixteen-round session in which the model simply stopped populating the namespace dict. A session pathology beat the PLM arm, not a reasoning deficit. Against my own case I will also record that those PLM arms never passed `constraint` and never made a protocol-bearing call, reading their answers out of the namespace after plain `react` rounds, so every loss on that side was authoring rather than a mechanism the contract had withheld.

Three constructed tasks stacked the mechanisms PLM has and the string contract does not, and none produced a gap I could defend. The first swung two thirds the wrong way on the small model and converged at ceiling on the larger one, where every arm finished perfect and the task stopped discriminating. The second scored zero everywhere, partly through difficulty and partly through a PLM-side degeneracy worth naming, one instance spending fourteen rounds politely refusing to call its own tool and asking me to run it and paste the result back. The third sat at chance in all four arms, and one of the four was RLM-shaped by construction, single-shot string calls over a context held as a REPL variable with no round budget exposed to the sub-call, so the comparison was never only against my own agentic region.

Two findings arrived unbidden, and both cut against the theory. A fresh context per phase acts as an error reset, deleting the degeneracy a long session accumulates, so at small scale the boundaries I had counted as losses compounded a cleansing; on the larger model the degeneracy is gone, the reset has nothing left to cure, and the sign moved without ever flipping my way. And curation is a liability when the distillation is noisy: the policy that pruned stale reports and told the model its state table was its memory scored zero, while the same composition with the raw reports left in the weave scored a third. The redundancy I removed was the redundancy the model repaired itself with.

One finding runs the other way, and it is about the protocol rather than the contract. On the qualifying model the naive bubble scored zero, piping its first observation straight into `RETURN` and quitting on all six instances, and the naive chopped arm scored 0.50, its bubbles satisfying the return obligation without performing the commit the task actually graded; ordinary code around the call repaired both to a clean sweep. The protocol tax is real, it is the only irreducible I saw bite at that scale, and it is the one most cheaply patched from outside. That is why the emulation ties.

The mechanism behind the null constrains everyone, not only me. Code computes whatever its inputs determine, which is why a solver inside a policy beats any amount of deliberation about the same clauses; but where a task's difficulty is judgment rather than computation, the model is the only source, and a policy's output distribution is a computable transformation of the conditionals it can elicit. A policy can select among samples, condition them differently, compose them, amplify them with a checker. It cannot create probability the model does not assign. At vanishing probability under every reachable conditioning, no shape helps; at decent probability, cheap shapes already win.

One thing escapes that argument, and it is a theorem rather than a hope. Give a policy a sound polynomial-time checker and $$k$$ samples, and its success on an obligation is one minus the chance of $$k$$ failures, so the region it handles reliably strictly contains the model's own and tends to the model's support as $$k$$ grows. A harness moves a task to where the model is reliable; a verifying policy only needs it possible. Verification is also the top rung of a ladder, voting, search, invariant regulation, cross-examination, constrained decoding beneath it, each demanding a weaker symbolic object, and a policy is the closure of those generators under composition, not the list. Every rung has an output-side form that any coded harness owns, about which I claim nothing, and a trajectory-side form that only a participant in the computation owns. My probes exercised the output side almost exclusively, which is consistent with the nulls, and is exactly where the theory says a gap should not be.

So, exactly: what fell is the prediction, that the larger authorable space shows up as an accuracy gap on tasks I could build and afford. The containment stands, an RLM decomposition is a PLM policy at the one level of nesting both systems actually run, call depth being fixed by my kernel as an experimental condition while orchestration depth stays unbounded on both sides. And the invariant stands. Sufficiency remains inexpressible under the string contract for the contract's own reason, and assimilation, which sufficiency licenses, is the part of this idea I still believe in.

## What has to be trained

Placed on the harness untrained, current models delegate but do not meta-reason, and the affinity improves little under prompting, even with detailed, optimized system prompts, reasoning models hold to the habits of the single trajectory. The disposition has to be trained in, and the training is not only subtractive. Taking work off the trajectory is half of it, the other half is new duties, authoring, granting, briefing, weaving, that no model has ever been trained to perform.

The original plan was to train a model that authors policies in a space whose coverage exceeds the decomposition space, and the summer removed the first half of that plan. What remains, and what I think is the more interesting target anyway, is training for the invariant: a model rewarded for keeping sufficiency and minimality together, which is to say rewarded for what it assimilates and for what it declines to carry. How meta-reasoning is enforced, how it is rewarded, how it is discovered at all, are open questions, and we leave them open deliberately. The substrate is more expressive than the one it is compared to, and that is exactly why it must be trained. Developing that training is the program of this work.

There is one direction I have not tested and want to. Because the harness is a participant in its own computation rather than a caller of it, the supervision available inside it is different in kind from task-level outcome: which branch verified, where an invariant broke and at which round, which continuation survived. Those labels are visible to a system that owns the trajectory and structurally invisible to one that does not. If contracts determine what supervision exists, then the contract question is a training question, and that is the version of this program I would most like to be wrong about in the other direction.

## Appendix: the experimental program

I am writing this before the program is finished, and the honest state is uneven, so here is what was planned, what has been run, and what has not.

**E1, the gradient.** Whether policy shape alone, holding the model and the task fixed, moves capability. This part came out clean. On the deduction task the same executor moved from 0.17 to 0.70 across policy designs, a factor of four from authoring alone, the endpoints coming from a six-instance baseline and a ten-instance final. Every number in this post is on the small model I develop against, not yet requalified at the scale I would use for a paper, so I report the gradient as a strong indication rather than a settled measurement. Zhang's harness results and the Meta-Harness line of work point the same way from a different direction.

**E2, the barrier.** Whether some task exists where the best policy in the full space beats the best policy under the string contract by a margin that survives a serious attempt at emulation. This part failed, and the verdicts are better named than counted. The duel came in under its own threshold with the gap running the wrong way. The first constructed task had its kill condition fire at two thirds in the wrong direction, then converged at ceiling on the larger model. The second was voided by a floor effect. The third was discarded on audit, a row-level check finding the small model mis-recording between half and two thirds of its own observations, so its four-way tie was noise. A fourth run, a composed policy stacking six mechanisms at once, was discarded before reporting when I found the comparison arm had no parse-retry loop while the constrained arm effectively did, an authoring asymmetry rather than a contract one. And the minimal exhibit for role authorship hit its pre-registered threshold from the wrong side, roles intact at twenty-four of twenty-four against the flattened version's twenty-two, a difference of 0.083 against a threshold of 0.10, so that axis is behaviorally inert on the shape I tested at the scale I tested it.

I want to be careful about how much all that means. It rules out the gaps I knew how to construct, on the tasks I could afford, at the scale I could afford. It does not rule out the regime I think the theory points at, window-pressured, long-horizon work where the eviction of one's own past reasoning becomes irreversible, though I am saying that after the null rather than before it, and it is the first thing I would want an unsympathetic reader to press on. I did not get to that regime, it is expensive, and time ran out before the budget did. One axis never got loaded at all, selective granting, and the reason is a design fact rather than a schedule: an agentic policy controls its own globals, so it can quarantine by keeping the sensitive object in a local variable and passing a sanitized string, which closes the naive version of that task before it starts.

**E3, authoring.** Whether models can author into this space at all, and how that ability varies with what the harness exposes. Not run. This is also the question Alex's work bears on most directly, since the compositional-generalization result is precisely about training a root to produce the right decompositions, and any serious version of E3 has to be read against it.

A caution about the method, which is the part of this I would defend regardless of the result. Two probes were discarded on audit rather than reported, one because the model was mis-recording most of its own observations and every arm was scoring at chance, and one because I had given the comparison arm a worse deal than the arm I wanted to win. Both would have been publishable-looking results. Neither was real, and the second was mine.
