## The trajectory

Frontier large language models and the harnesses built around them can now tackle complex long-horizon problems, resolve software issues, conduct deep research, and drive hours-long sessions. The gains that enabled this have largely arrived through inference-time scaling. Models are trained to spend more tokens deliberating, and ReAct systems extend those capabilities by interleaving reasoning with actions and observations. ReAct remains the dominant substrate for this work, and a ReAct trajectory is a growing sequence of those thoughts, actions, and observations.

The trajectory only grows, every thought, action, and observation is appended, and LLM outputs degrade as the context window fills. The most popular general remedy is context compaction, where the trajectory is repeatedly summarized when it exceeds a length threshold. Compaction loses information, and it does not repair the reasoning inside the window. Trajectories pushed to length fail in characteristic ways, exploration that fragments the reasoning, self-reflection that strains against load-bearing mistakes, the capacity of the window itself; the short version is a single mismatch. The model repeatedly reaches for reasoning shapes the autoregressive substrate cannot hold: it branches to weigh alternatives, reverts to undo a wrong step, and explores before committing, yet every such move is flattened back into one forward sequence. I think these failures act as a dampener on reasoning LLMs, and that solving them will yield far more capable reasoners.

The failures are not only about length. A ReAct loop bundles two separable things, the capability of interleaving thought with code, and the protocol that ends the call: a disclosed round budget, an obligation to answer, a return channel, and pressure at the end of the budget. Every framework ships them fused, because a call that cannot end is useless to a caller who only gets a return value. I found in my own runs that the protocol has its own failure modes, distinct from any reasoning deficit. Sub-agents surrender early and return nothing, or return a progress report where an answer belonged, or return the literal string `done`. These are artifacts of the channel rather than of the deduction, and they show up wherever the channel is present.

## Taking work off the line

Recently, [Zhang, Kraska, and Khattab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24601) introduced Recursive Language Models (RLMs), a general-purpose inference paradigm for dramatically scaling the effective input and output lengths of LLMs. Their key insight is that an arbitrarily long prompt should not be fed into the neural network directly, but treated as part of the environment that the LLM symbolically and recursively interacts with. Given a prompt $$P$$, an RLM initializes a REPL environment in which $$P$$ is set as the value of a variable, and the model writes code that peeks into and decomposes $$P$$, observes the side effects of execution, and invokes the LLM itself on as many slices of the input as necessary.

I first read this as a result about input processing, and said so in the earlier version of this post. That was a misreading of the claim, and Zhang's [work over the summer](https://alexzhang13.github.io/blog/2026/harness/) makes the actual claim explicit. The claim is about composition. A harness induces an equivalence relation over tasks: two problems that share latent structure, however different their vocabulary and their length, are reduced to nearly the same token trajectory in the root, because the harness offloads the domain-specific material into sub-calls and exposes only the structure needed to choose the next operation. A good harness shapes each call so that every observation is, in his phrase, locally in-distribution. The consequence he demonstrates is that training on short tasks generalizes to tasks eight to thirty-two times longer, and that training on one domain transfers to others. Input length is where this is measured. It is not what is being claimed.

This matters for me because it is the strongest available statement of what a harness is for, and because it is not the statement I was making. His invariant is a property of the model's input distribution, that every call the model sees looks like something it was trained on. Mine is a property of the trajectory's information state. Those are different objects, and most of this post is about the second one. It is worth adding that he hedges the reach of his own conjecture, that the tasks people care about can "apparently almost always" be decomposed naturally, and he offers no characterization of the training set that would suffice. I read those hedges as honest rather than as an opening.

## Policy Language Models

We introduce Policy Language Models (PLMs), a general-purpose meta-reasoning inference paradigm for dramatically scaling the reasoning power of LLMs. The key insight is that reasoning to generate computations, computations that do tasks or find answers or reveal information, gives rise to meta-reasoning. This behavior sheds the failures plaguing ReAct while keeping all its benefits, offers additional benefits, and, I believe, carries a far higher ceiling for reasoning LLMs. We define meta-reasoning as the behavior of identifying problems, reasoning out the computation that solves them, and assimilating its work. A PLM is made of two parts: the harness, where computations are authored and run as policies, and the model, trained to reason them out.

Assimilation is the word that carries the most weight there, and it is the part of this program that survived everything below. A PLM does not merely dispatch work, it takes the work back, and the trajectory that authored a policy is also the trajectory that reads what the policy produced, decides what it means, and reasons onward from it. The harness is built so that nothing produced anywhere in the computation is out of that trajectory's reach.

What an RLM does for its input, a PLM does for all its computation. Instead of reasoning and computing inline, a PLM authors policies: programmatic artifacts it can run, edit, and compose, and lets them carry the work a trajectory would otherwise hold: the mechanical steps, the reasoning, the interaction with the world. Policies live and execute in a persistent REPL environment. Our harness also provides sub-LLM functional policies, so a PLM can create policies capable of reasoning and computation. The PLM can control what a policy returns: our harness's constraint abstraction lets it fix the invariants of any policy's output, the sub-LLM policies included. The REPL is the only way a PLM acts; it is, however, not confined to only authoring policies there; it can inspect, compute, and act directly, much as an RLM does. What makes it a meta-reasoner is not a prohibition on doing the work itself but a disposition, trained into it, to author policies that do the work better than it would inline.

The trajectory itself is responsible for abstract reasoning. There the PLM reasons over the goal and authors the policies that set its plans in motion: policies that test its hypotheses, that process its inputs, that write and emit the code it owes. A PLM's job is to reason, and it engineers its instruments with an understanding of the goal, sees the work they did, and reasons onward. And since reading an input is itself computation, the RLM capability comes along as a special case, a long input can sit in the environment and be worked through with policies.

What the substrate buys is compounding. A policy authored once can be rerun, revised, and composed into larger ones, so the work of reasoning accumulates as artifacts rather than as tokens. The substrate alone, however, does not produce the behavior. In the observational runs I did before this program, current LLMs placed on the PLM harness did not spontaneously meta-reason, they ground the work inline out of habit, or scattered one-off delegations that were at best half-cooked. The disposition has to be trained in. That is what I take from it, and it is why the rest of this is about training.

## The harness

A Policy Language Model is a large language model that meta-reasons: it reasons about the goal on its own trajectory and expresses the computation as policies in a persistent programming environment $$\mathcal{E}$$. Each round the model thinks abstractly, then acts through one code tool call that executes in $$\mathcal{E}$$ and updates its state. A PLM trajectory holds only three things: the abstract reasoning, the code, and the code's output as the tool result.

It is more honest to say the harness has four axes than to say it has three primitives, because what a program controls is the conversation a generation conditions on, how persistent context evolves between generations, the law the output is sampled from, and which model continues a given context. `llm`, `react` and `react_auto` are points in that lattice rather than the capabilities themselves. Saying it that way answers the obvious objection, that I picked the wrong base capabilities, since the axes are the base and the primitives are bundles of them.

Meta-reasoning is the axis of inference along which the model reasons about the goal and reasons about generating the computations that will help attain it. It holds together abstract reasoning about the goal with the design of compute structures for the tasks that goal breaks into, and what binds them is the cycle where the model decides what to do and how to do it, inlines the task or expresses it as computation, takes back what the computation produced, and reasons on.

A policy is any computation the PLM expresses rather than performs. The definition is behavioral, not syntactic, and the harness gives it support: the `@policy` decorator turns a function or a class into a named artifact that persists across rounds, holds global scope so it is used by name and never imported, and can be edited in place and duplicated. Authored once, it can be rerun, edited, and composed into larger policies.

The harness gives the PLM its LLM policies to build with. `llm` is a single call. `react_auto` is an LLM with the python tool, run in ReAct rounds under the termination protocol, and it is what a conventional sub-agent is. `react` is the same loop with the protocol removed: it runs exactly the rounds it is given and stops, still open, and the name `RETURN` does not exist inside it. The split is deliberate and it came out of the failures above. Termination becomes a decision the policy can make from outside, having read what the sub-computation actually did, rather than a decision the sub-model must make under deadline pressure. `react` injects no text of its own, no budget, no round count, no briefing about its own mechanism, so every word in a stepped trajectory is either the author's or the model's.

These are the bare minimum on their own, and can be used to build arbitrarily complex information processing circuits. The building, however, is deliberate work: every sub-llm call begins empty. Every input, helper, prompt, any policy it will use, the `@policy` decorator and the LLM policies themselves, are handed to it. Nothing about a sub-llm is ambient, so a working one has to be constructed. Fanning out is never a reflex but a decision the PLM has to make and build, so it decomposes only when it decides to. Computation can never be fully controlled or predicted, but a harness decides who holds what control there is. Ours concentrates it in the PLM.

Constraints and weaving complete the harness. A constraint is a way to define invariants over a computation's output, authored as a typed class. It is worth being precise about what it does, because the name undersells it: a constraint is compiled into the schema the call decodes under, so the model cannot emit a violating structure in the first place. This is a different operation from validating a returned string and asking again, which resamples the same unconstrained distribution. An LLM's judgment so becomes a typed value in ordinary control flow, `react_auto("...", constraint=Bool)` yields a validated value or `None` if the budget runs out without one, so policy code branches on the result and is if-shaped rather than try-shaped, which is deliberate: a failure to deliver is a value, not an exception.

Weaving is what makes the trajectory and the namespace ordinary objects. Messages pass by reference, so a `react` run over a list leaves its turns on that list, and the list can be handed onward, edited, pruned, or restored, never as pasted text. The sub-agent's namespace is a dictionary the caller owns, so everything the sub-computation defined, its variables and the functions it wrote, is readable and callable by the policy after the call and between rounds. Granting is putting a key in that dictionary. There is no session object anywhere: a session is the pair of containers the author is holding. Branching that namespace needs one piece of care I had to measure before I believed it, since `deepcopy` treats functions as atomic and leaves a copied namespace's functions bound to the parent's globals, so a branch calling an inherited helper read the parent's data and drove the parent's counter while its own stayed at zero, and the harness therefore ships a `copy_namespace` that rebuilds the sub-agent's own functions over the child dictionary.

## The invariant

Here is the thing this program is actually about, and it is worth stating as two conditions rather than one sentence.

**D1, sufficiency.** At any point of the root trajectory, nothing the trajectory has produced becomes unrecoverable, whether or not the reasoner knew at the time that it would matter. The root need not carry the information itself. It must carry either the information, or information about the information, enough to recover it on demand from the environment. Assimilation is what this condition licenses: work can leave the trajectory without leaving the reasoner's reach.

**D2, minimality.** Subject to D1, the root trajectory carries as little as possible. Every token on it should be reasoning about the goal, not the material the reasoning was performed on.

Meta-reasoning is the disposition that preserves D1 and D2 together across the cycles of authoring, running, and assimilating. Stating them as a pair is the point. A long-context model has sufficiency without minimality, everything is in one window and nothing is recoverable once evicted. A system that offloads aggressively has minimality without sufficiency, the root stays short but a sub-computation's reasoning is gone the moment it returns a string. Zhang's invariant, that every observation be locally in-distribution, is a statement about what the model must generalize over, and it is a good one. It is not a statement about what the root can still reach.

An RLM cannot express D1, and the check is the contract rather than a benchmark: `rlm_query` returns a string, and there is no channel that returns the turns. A sub-call's trajectory is structurally invisible to the root, which sees only the string the sub-model chose to return, where a PLM policy holds that trajectory as an ordinary list, every turn the sub-model wrote, its code and its answers. Neither system sees the hidden reasoning tokens, and my own harness strips that field before the wire, so the comparison is over what actually crosses it. His own observation that RLM roots keep printing task-specific information back into the main context, which he notes is undesirable from a generalization standpoint, is the system straining against exactly this missing channel.

The other half of the contract argument is role authorship, that under the string contract the mapping from provenance to role is fixed by the protocol while a PLM policy places any content under any role, an assistant turn the model appears to have committed to, a tool result the environment appears to have produced, a system update arriving mid-history.

## What I set out to show, and what happened

The theory above has a companion claim that seemed obvious when I started, and it was the claim I spent the summer trying to establish. If a PLM can hold objects an RLM cannot, then the space of policies a PLM can author should be strictly larger in a way that shows up as capability. Formally, write $$\Pi_X$$ for the interaction structures a program can control under contract $$X$$, and $$R(\pi)$$ for the set of tasks a policy $$\pi$$ gets right. The meta-reasoner's job is authoring, so what a trained PLM covers is the union of $$R(\pi)$$ over the policies $$\theta$$ can write. That is the theta question, and the bet was that this union is larger than what a decomposition-shaped system reaches, which would have been a direct challenge to Zhang's conjecture.

I built three harnesses to test it. One is the full PLM harness. One is the same harness with exactly the beyond-string-contract surface removed, so a policy there is arbitrary code around string-in, string-out calls, which is the agentic region, and which becomes the RLM region when the round budget is fixed, the system message withheld, and the namespace switch turned off. The third is a further restricted variant for a separate experiment. The diff between them is asserted by a test, so the boundary is materialized rather than promised.

Then I ran the comparison, and it did not go the way I expected. The short version is that I could not find the capability gap. On an interactive deduction task, both sides were maximized honestly and the best agentic policy scored 0.70 against the best PLM policy's 0.60 over ten development instances, a difference of one instance, and pooled over every run of that task the agentic arm sits at 0.80 against the PLM arm's 0.53 with enough run-to-run variance that the best PLM arm scored five of six and then two of five on overlapping instances. I should add the number that makes both of those figures nearly meaningless as a barrier test, which is that an offline pass over all ten knowledge books, done before any policy ran, put the task's own ceiling at 0.616 under its action budget and 0.662 unbudgeted, because the books over-claim and the observations rule out the gold artifact in seven of the ten instances. Both arms were already pressed against a ceiling set by noise in the data rather than by either harness contract.

The emulation is the part I found hardest to argue with. I wrote the best flattened version of the PLM policy I could, everything the PLM arm did but re-fed as one labeled string under the string contract, and it reproduced the PLM maximum exactly on the iteration set and then beat it on the full ten, at sixty-four percent of that arm's token cost. I also instrumented both arms and graded every rule they compiled against a reference table, which is where the result stops being a score and becomes a mechanism: re-feeding read the books at ninety-one percent exact-rule fidelity, exactly what the live agentic session read them at, while the best PLM arm sat at 77.2 percent, having lost thirty-eight rules. Thirty-six of those thirty-eight came from one instance where fidelity fell to 5.3 percent, deep into a session of sixteen rounds where the model simply stopped populating the namespace dict, so what beat the PLM arm here was a single session pathology and not a reasoning deficit.

Against my own case I should also record that the constraint did no work in the runs that lost, since the PLM arms of that duel never passed `constraint` at all and never made a protocol-bearing call either, reading their answers out of the namespace after plain `react` rounds. Every loss on that side was authoring rather than a mechanism the contract had withheld.

On three constructed tasks designed specifically to stack the mechanisms PLM has and the string contract does not, none produced a gap I could defend. On the first, a gap of two thirds in the wrong direction on the small model closed to nothing on the larger one, where every arm finished at ceiling and the task no longer discriminated at all. The second ran and scored zero on every instance, partly because its three conjunctive clauses were too hard for the small model and partly because of a PLM-side degeneracy worth naming, fourteen rounds on one instance of a model politely refusing to call its own tool and asking me to run it and paste the result back. On the third, all four arms sat at chance, and one of those four was RLM-shaped by construction, single-shot string calls over a context held as a REPL variable with no round budget exposed to the sub-call, so the comparison was not only against my own agentic region.

Two things arrived unbidden and both cut against the theory. The mechanism at the small scale was not continuity loss at all, it was that a fresh context per phase acts as an error reset which deletes the degeneracy a long session accumulates, and on the larger model that degeneracy is gone, the same arm commits soundly and solves every instance, so the reset has nothing left to cure and the sign moved without ever flipping in my favour. And curation turns out to be a liability when the distillation is noisy, since the policy that pruned stale reports and told the model its state table was its durable memory scored zero, while the same composition with the raw reports left in the weave scored a third, the redundancy the model needed to repair its own bookkeeping having been precisely what the pruning removed.

There is one positive finding in the wreckage and it is about the protocol rather than the contract. On the qualifying model the naive single-bubble arm scored zero because it piped its first observation straight into `RETURN` and quit on all six instances, the naive chopped arm scored 0.50 because its bubbles satisfied the return obligation without performing the commit the task actually graded, and both were repaired to a clean sweep by ordinary code written around the call. The protocol tax is real and it is the only irreducible I saw bite at that scale. It is also the one most cheaply patched from outside, which is why the emulation still ties.

The mechanism behind the null is not mysterious, and it is worth stating plainly because it constrains everyone, not only me. Code computes whatever its inputs determine, and it does so far better than the model can inline, which is why a solver inside a policy beats any amount of deliberation about the same clauses. But where the difficulty of a task is judgment rather than computation, the model is the only source, and there a policy's output distribution is a computable transformation of the conditionals it can elicit. A policy can select among samples, condition them differently, compose them, and amplify them with a checker. It cannot create probability the model does not assign. Where a task sits at vanishingly small probability under every reachable conditioning, no shape helps. Where it sits at decent probability, cheap shapes already win.

One thing does escape that argument and I want it on the record, because it is a theorem rather than a hope. Give a policy a sound checker it can run in polynomial time and $$k$$ samples to spend, and its success on an obligation is one minus the chance of $$k$$ failures, so the region of obligations it handles reliably strictly contains the model's own and tends to the model's support as $$k$$ grows. A harness moves a task into the region where the model is reliable; a verifying policy only needs the region where the model is possible. That is a property of the policy, the model and the task together, and it does not reduce to the model's distribution. Verification is also not the only shape, it is the strongest rung of a ladder that holds voting, search, invariant regulation, cross-examination and constrained decoding below it, each needing a weaker symbolic object than the one above, and what a policy can be is the closure of those generators under composition rather than the list itself. Every rung comes in two forms, an output-side form applied to returned values, which any harness with code has and which I claim nothing about, and a trajectory-side form applied to the live computation. The probes and the duel exercised the output-side forms almost exclusively, which is consistent with the nulls and is exactly where the theory says a gap should not be.

I should be exact about what did and did not fall. What fell is the prediction, that the larger authorable space would show up as an accuracy gap on tasks I could build and afford. The structural result did not fall: the containment is constructive, an RLM decomposition is a PLM policy at the single level of nesting both systems actually run, and my harness fixes that depth as an experimental condition rather than as a claim about what the space contains, which is milder than it sounds because call depth is one on both sides while orchestration depth is unbounded on both. The invariant did not fall either. D1 remains inexpressible under the string contract, for the reason given above, and assimilation, which is what D1 licenses, is the part of this idea I still believe in.

## What has to be trained

Placed on the harness untrained, current models delegate but do not meta-reason, and the affinity improves little under prompting, even with detailed, optimized system prompts, reasoning models hold to the habits of the single trajectory. The disposition has to be trained in, and the training is not only subtractive. Taking work off the trajectory is half of it, the other half is new duties, authoring, granting, briefing, weaving, that no model has ever been trained to perform.

The original plan was to train a model that authors policies in a space whose coverage exceeds the decomposition space, on the theory that a larger space plus a model trained to reach into it yields a system that solves what neither reaches alone. The first half of that plan is what the summer removed. What remains, and what I think is the more interesting target anyway, is training for the invariant: a model rewarded for keeping D1 and D2 together, which is to say rewarded for what it assimilates and for what it declines to carry. How that is enforced, how it is rewarded, and how it is discovered at all remain open, and I leave them open deliberately.

There is one direction I have not tested and want to. Because the harness is a participant in its own computation rather than a caller of it, the supervision available inside it is different in kind from task-level outcome: which branch verified, where an invariant broke and at which round, which continuation survived. Those labels are visible to a system that owns the trajectory and structurally invisible to one that does not. If contracts determine what supervision exists, then the contract question is a training question, and that is the version of this program I would most like to be wrong about in the other direction.

## Appendix: the experimental program

I am writing this before the program is finished, and the honest state is uneven, so here is what was planned, what has been run, and what has not.

**E1, the gradient.** Whether policy shape alone, holding the model and the task fixed, moves capability. This is the part that came out clean. On the deduction task the same executor moved from 0.17 to 0.70 across policy designs, a factor of four from authoring alone, though the endpoints come from a six-instance baseline and a ten-instance final rather than one set. Every number in this appendix and the section above is on the small model I develop against, and I have not yet requalified any of them at the scale I would use for a paper, so I report the gradient as a strong indication rather than a settled measurement. Zhang's harness results and the Meta-Harness line of work point the same way from a different direction.

**E2, the barrier.** Whether there is a task where the best policy in the full space beats the best policy under the string contract by a margin that survives a serious attempt at emulation. This is the part that failed. The verdicts, rather than a tally: the duel came in under its own threshold with the gap running the wrong way; the first constructed task had its kill condition fire at two thirds in the wrong direction and then converged at ceiling on the larger model; the second was voided by a floor effect; the third was discarded on audit after a row-level check found the small model mis-recording between half and two thirds of its own observations, which meant its four-way tie was noise; a fourth, a composed policy stacking six mechanisms at once, was discarded before reporting when I found the comparison arm had no parse-retry loop while the constrained arm effectively did, which is an authoring asymmetry rather than a contract one; and the minimal exhibit for role authorship hit its pre-registered threshold from the wrong side, roles intact at twenty-four of twenty-four against the flattened version's twenty-two, a difference of 0.083 against a threshold of 0.10, so that axis is behaviorally inert on the shape I tested at the scale I tested it.

I want to be careful about how much that means. It rules out the gaps I knew how to construct, on the tasks I could afford to run, at the model scale I could afford to run them. It does not rule out the regime I think the theory points at, which is window-pressured, long-horizon work where the eviction of one's own past reasoning becomes irreversible, though I should say plainly that I am saying so after the null rather than before it, and it is the first thing I would want an unsympathetic reader to press on. I did not get to that regime, it is expensive, and time ran out before the budget did. One of the four axes I meant to stack never got loaded at all, which is selective granting, and the reason is a design fact rather than a schedule: an agentic policy controls its own globals, so it can quarantine by keeping the sensitive object in a local variable and passing a sanitized string, which closes the naive version of that task before it starts.

**E3, authoring.** Whether models can author into this space at all, and how that ability varies with what the harness exposes. Not run. This is also the question Alex's work bears on most directly, since the compositional-generalization result is precisely about training a root to produce the right decompositions, and any serious version of E3 has to be read against it.

A caution about the method, which is the part of this I would defend regardless of the result. Two probes were discarded on audit rather than reported, one because the model was mis-recording most of its own observations and every arm was therefore scoring at chance, and one because I had given the comparison arm a worse deal than the arm I wanted to win. Both would have been publishable-looking results. Neither was real, and the second was mine.
