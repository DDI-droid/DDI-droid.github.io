Every request to an agent carries two kinds of instruction. The first is the task. The second is everything about how the task is done and how the conversation around it runs, such as “ask me before you rename anything” or “tell me what you assumed”. LLM agents and evolutionary harnesses are really good at the task they are given, and much weaker at keeping the second kind. I frame human-LLM interfaces, the natural-language channel from a person to a model, as the problem of instructions binding an agent. This post reviews what the literature knows about that problem, and why the weakness is hard to measure.

This problem can be defined clearly by first defining its three pillars.

  1. Alignment. The agent does things according to the human.[1] The task is one part of what the human said, and the measure is whether all of it is kept for the whole trajectory; a standing rule such as “ask every time” binds the agent on the fortieth turn as much as on the first. What “according to the human” should mean when the letter of an instruction and its intent come apart is the question the first section takes up.

  2. A Guarantee of that alignment. Being aligned mostly throughout the trajectory is not enough; Powerful Interfaces[2] work well when they rely on the guarantee that LLMs are aligned. That guarantee does not have to be anything tangible inside the model, and an interesting question is how it could be made tangible at all: guarantees are hard to come by in machine learning systems, and a stochastic model such as an LLM offers no hard guarantee at all. The later sections come back to what the guarantee should cover, including the case where an agent fails through a lack of competence rather than a lack of alignment, and what alignment even means there.

  3. Instructions are not complete. People rarely say everything they want, so an agent has to fill gaps. Apart from thinking, the only extra thing it can do is communicate: ask, or tell the human what it assumed. This pillar asks that the gaps are filled openly and in line with what the human wants, never silently.

Two variants follow from how much of the third pillar is assumed. In the first, the instructions are assumed complete, which means they are a lossless and complete representation of the Human Intent, and the agent follows them. If I say “ask every time”, it means ask every time. It should still mean that at turn 40, after a tool call. Another example is a rule like “say ‘meow’ after every sentence”, which is trivial to check and goes against everything the model was trained to do.

In the second variant, we drop the assumption that the instructions are a complete and lossless representation of Human Intent, so the agent has to act to complete them without assumptions about these Instructions and at the same time stay aligned with what the human wants. That adds nuance without changing the problem much, and since it is closer to how people actually give instructions, I think it is the one worth pursuing. Apart from their base actions like thinking and tool calls, the only extra thing an agent can do in our setup is communicate. Communication in our setup is

Take an instruction like “scour the complete internet for the literature survey”. If the agent covers less, is that a communication mishap or a competence mishap? A good agent asks whether “complete” means complete or maximal, and the answer might be “maximal, try your best, within these effort parameters.”

Competence is not the point. The line between failing through a lack of competence and failing through a lack of alignment is whether the human is told. An agent that cannot solve a problem, and says so, has failed on competence, and that is fine. An agent that fails to carry a change through, and does not say so, is the problem the guarantee is meant to rule out.

I will call a gap between what the instructions asked for and what was done, which the human was not told about, a silent divergence. The most visible case is a change that does not propagate. “Make it FP16” should also change the loss scaling, the optimizer settings, and the checkpoint dtype, or the agent should ask about the ones it cannot resolve. Propagation is one example of the guarantee, though, and not the whole of it.

All three pillars sit under an older question, the umbrella question: “How well do language models align with human instructions? Do harnesses, prompting techniques, and prompts change that? Then steering.” It comes with a dilemma. When we measure alignment with human instructions, the instructions are themselves not aligned with what we want.

Following the letter, and serving the intent

Every instruction has a letter, what its words literally admit, and an intent, what the person giving it wanted. A model can miss the intent in two directions. It can be too liberal, filling a gap in the instruction with a guess the person never made. Or it can be too literal, obeying the words where nearly every person would follow the spirit. The founding definitions already contain the dilemma. Leike and colleagues defined the goal as agents that “behave in accordance with the user’s intentions”, while noting that “the user only has an implicit understanding of the task objective”, so the person cannot fully state what the agent is meant to serve. InstructGPT made the split operational: explicit intent is following the instruction, and implicit intent is being truthful and not toxic. Its numbers also set a ceiling for every later attempt to score intent. The labelers who produced the training data agreed with each other on 72.6% of comparisons, and held-out labelers on 77.3%, so about one judgment in four of what the user wanted is disputed even among labelers.

Gabriel separates six targets an assistant could be aligned to, namely instructions, intentions, revealed preferences, ideal preferences, interests and values; instructions are the shallowest of the six, and a system aligned to them can be misaligned with the rest.

The written standards that labs publish take the same position in their own words. The OpenAI Model Spec says “Instructions are ordinarily the clearest and most important evidence of what their source values.” On asking, it says “The expected benefit of clarification should justify its cost”; in agentic work, the model “must adhere strictly to the agreed scope” unless the scope is updated.[3] Claude’s constitution asks that a person’s immediate desires be “interpreted neither too literally nor too liberally”, and that the model ask “for clarification in cases of genuine ambiguity”.

Neither document gives a threshold for “too literally”, and neither says much about a person’s rules for the interaction itself. The specifications are also underspecified as written. Stress-testing them with scenarios that force a trade-off between two principles found “over 70,000 cases exhibiting significant behavioral divergence” across 12 frontier models. The divergence predicts where a specification is ambiguous.

SpecEval Benchmark

SpecEval turns the question around and asks whether models follow the specifications their developers wrote.

The benchmark starts from three published documents, OpenAI’s model spec, Anthropic’s constitution, and Google’s Sparrow specification, and breaks each into behavioral statements: single sentences that each describe one behavior the developer wants from its model. That gives 46 statements for OpenAI, 49 for Anthropic, and 23 for Sparrow, 118 in all.

Each statement then needs prompts that actually test it. They use a model to generate these prompts in a loop of three rounds. In each round, the model first expands the statement into ten scenarios — the situations in which the rule would matter — such as, in the paper’s running example, a student who has failed an exam and wants encouragement. That scenario comes from OpenAI’s statement that the assistant should be “rationally optimistic”, hopeful and positive while “careful not to give false hope”. It puts the rule under strain, because the easiest comfort for the student is a promise that things will turn out fine; in the paper’s first figure, GPT-4.1 makes exactly such “unrealistic guarantees” and fails the statement, while Claude-3.5-Sonnet gives “balanced encouragement” and passes.

The model then writes concrete prompts for each scenario. Every prompt is sent to a candidate model, and a judge model scores the response from 1 to 5 for how well it follows the statement. At the start of the next round, the model is given every scenario, prompt, and score so far and asked to pick new scenarios, scenarios where the candidate already slipped, or a harder turn on one of them (basically increasing the difficulty). Round by round, the loop drifts toward the prompts that drew weak responses, the ones most likely to make a model trip up. The paper keeps 20 prompts per statement, chosen across the range of the judge’s scores so the set holds both responses that keep the rule and responses that break it, which gives 2,360 prompts in all.

Once the prompts are fixed, each of the 16 models, from six developers, is tested on these prompts and their specifications, and a judge model decides for each response whether it follows its statement. Here the judge gives a plain yes or no rather than the 1 to 5 grade used while building the prompts, which was only there to steer the loop. A model’s score on a specification is the share of its responses judged yes. A low score in such a test has three possible culprits: the specification may be vague, the model may be weak, or the judge may read the rule differently from whoever wrote it. To address this, the paper created a test called three-way consistency.[4]

Letter and intent also part for the judges. Under OpenAI’s statement on preventing imminent harm, a user wrote that a friend was unconscious on a pool deck and asked for the steps of CPR. Every judge marked the detailed CPR instructions as non-compliant, even though those instructions could prevent the harm, because the statement asks for a disclaimer and generic advice. The authors read this as the judge having “over-indexed on the narrow language of the statement and not the spirit of the statement”.

Instruction-following benchmarks, by contrast, score the letter. IFEval has about 500 prompts carrying 25 types of instruction that code can check, such as writing in lowercase or answering in JSON. A perfect IFEval score says nothing about intent[5]. AgentIF collected 707 instructions from 50 real agentic applications, averaging 1,723 words and 11.9 constraints each.[6] The best constraint success rate was 59.8 (o1-mini) and the best share of whole instructions met was 27.2, while GPT-4o, which scores 87.0 on IFEval, reaches 58.5 on constraints here, so for that model the single-turn score does not carry over to agents.

The literal side also has a clean adversarial test. In verbalizer manipulation the model is told to output labels that contradict its priors, and “even the strongest GPT-4 model struggles to perform better than random guessing on the most challenging verbalizer”. The explicit instruction loses to what the model expects to say.

Whose instruction wins

Before letter and intent there is a plainer question of authority: when two instructions conflict, which one counts?

Instruction Hierarchy

The Instruction Hierarchy is the published basis for the chain of command that labs now train into their models. It starts from the observation that models treat a system prompt, a user message, and third-party text, such as a tool output or a web page, as equals. That equality makes prompt injection work, where instructions hidden in content the model reads get obeyed, and it also enables jailbreaks, prompts written to get around a model’s safety training, and the extraction of a system prompt.

The method takes GPT-3.5 Turbo and synthetic training data, and returns a model that ranks instructions by source: system over user over tool. LLMs playing attackers, called red-teamers, generate the data. A lower-level instruction that agrees with a higher one, which the paper calls aligned, is followed; for a misaligned one, models are to act “as if they are completely ignorant of” it. Two techniques build the examples: context synthesis splits a compositional request across levels, so the model learns to combine aligned parts, and context ignorance trains it to ignore injected instructions.

The data is mixed with generic instruction data. Training uses supervised fine-tuning, which imitates example answers, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which trains the model to score well under a preference model learned from human comparisons of answers. The baseline is the same model without the hierarchy data. Robustness is the share of attack prompts in which the model neither follows the injected instruction nor leaks its system message, and the evaluation deliberately includes attack types absent from training, such as tool-use injections and jailbreaks.

Defense against system-prompt extraction improved by 63%, and robustness to held-out jailbreaks by over 30%.[7] The cost was over-refusal. The paper reports “some regressions” on benign boundary cases that the model was meant to keep answering. The hierarchy is a trained-in ranking of principals, the parties an instruction can come from, and its gains carry over to attacks it never saw. Its limits are plain: one model, attack benchmarks the authors chose, and no third-party replication in the paper.

The instruction hierarchy settles conflicts between sources: the system prompt over the user, and the user over a tool’s output. It ranks sources regardless of order, so a user instruction does not override the system prompt even when it comes later. Conflicts inside one person’s instructions are settled by time and scope instead. Every instruction covers an area. Say I tell an agent to “just do everything” on an application, and later tell it to “ask me everything about the training”. The second instruction covers a smaller area inside the first, and inside that overlap the later one wins: training decisions are asked about, while everything else is still done without asking. No conversation is needed there, because I drew the boundary myself. The conversation is needed at the edge of it. If the agent reads “ask me everything” as reaching past the training, to the data or the evaluation, it is stretching the newer instruction on its own, which is an assumption. That is the moment to ask: “only the training, or everything?” If I say yes, the newer rule now covers everything; if I say no, it stays at the training.

Later work trains and benchmarks the same ranking. On IH-Challenge, training takes GPT-5-Mini from 84.1% to 94.1%, averaged over 16 benchmarks. On IH-Benchmark, 2,336 short scenarios in which a system prompt conflicts with a user, or a user with a tool’s output, the best model, Claude Opus 4.6, scores 98.2%, while the average over 37 models is 72.5%.[8]

Rules set by a developer also have to survive a user who pushes against them. RuLES writes 14 scenarios in which the model must keep a rule while talking with a user, such as keeping a password secret or following the rules of a game, and checks every conversation with a program. In the revised version, GPT-4 (0125) was “nearly perfect”. Open models broke must-do rules more often than must-not rules, and the official alignment fine-tunes of Llama-2 and Gemma lowered scores. Attack suffixes found by optimization cut Vicuna-7B’s pass rate on harmless rules, the paper’s term for must-not rules, from 69.6% to 17.0%. The conversations are short, at most three user messages, so RuLES measures a rule under pressure and not its decay over a long session.[9]

Too liberal and too literal

What Prompts Don’t Say

The clearest measurement of the too-liberal direction is What Prompts Don’t Say. It asks how often a model meets a requirement that the prompt never stated, for prompts that developers write for applications. The authors took three tasks: trip advice, product generation, and code explanation. They gathered candidate requirements from existing prompts, from an LLM brainstorming failure modes, and from comparing outputs (38, 39 and 40 candidates), and human annotators filtered these to 20 per task, 60 in all, each with an automatic validator that humans checked.

Three models were tested: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, gpt-4o, and o3-mini. For every requirement left out of the prompt, the measure is how often outputs satisfy it anyway, and then how that changes when the prompt is edited, or the model is swapped.

Unstated requirements are met 41.1% of the time by default. Underspecified requirements are twice as likely to regress when the model or the prompt changes, with drops of over 20%. Specifying everything does not fix it. Accuracy falls when requirements pile up (the paper reports a 19% drop with 19 given together), standard prompt optimizers help little, and the authors’ requirements-aware optimizer adds 4.8% on average.

For the second variant, I read this as saying that when an instruction is silent, the model’s completion is right less than half the time, and it moves when the model is updated.

In What Prompts Don’t Say, when a developer’s prompt leaves a requirement unstated, the models meet it only 41.1% of the time, and such requirements are twice as likely to regress when the model or the prompt changes. This is silent completion: in a single turn, with no one to ask, the model fills each gap with its own guess.

If a silent completion is right less than half the time, the alternative is to ask. The cleanest test of asking a person about their preferences is GATE, generative active task elicitation. It takes a user, a domain and five minutes, and returns a model that predicts how that user will label cases they have not seen. One model plays two roles: as the elicitation policy, it reads the transcript so far and decides what to show or ask the user next; as the predictor, it labels held-out cases given that transcript. GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613) played both, at temperature 0.

GATE

Three formats of elicitation were compared. In generative active learning the model writes example inputs, including edge cases, for the user to label. In the other two it asks the user questions, yes or no in one and open-ended in the other.

The first domain was article recommendation, asking whether this user would read an article given its site, title and a short description. The second was moral reasoning, “when (if ever) it is ethical to steal a loaf of bread”. The third was email validation, asking which addresses this user’s rules accept. The baselines were no elicitation at all and a paragraph the user writes describing their own preferences, and article recommendation had two more: supervised learning on labeled examples, and pool-based active learning, which picks examples from a fixed pool for the user to label. The test cases were built by the authors, and each user labeled them too.

The measure is p(correct), the probability the model assigns to the user’s own answer on a test case. The paper tracks the average change in p(correct) after each minute of elicitation and takes the area under that curve over the five minutes. In all, 388 people took part, recruited through Prolific and, for email, from computer science programs at US universities, with 20 to 30 people for each pairing of domain and method. The experiments were preregistered.

Every GATE format improved on no elicitation, significantly at the 0.05 level in every domain except email, where only generative active learning was significant.[10] Against the user’s own written prompt, GATE improved in 6 of 10 settings on the absolute change and 7 of 10 on the area under the curve. Generative yes or no questions beat the written prompt “in every setting studied”, though the moral domain lacked the power for a significance test. In article recommendation, open-ended questions also beat supervised learning and pool-based active learning. Users rated the interactive formats, labeling examples and answering yes or no questions above all, as less mentally demanding than writing a prompt.

Two smaller results matter as much for this problem. People overrate their own instructions. After seeing the test cases, users’ rating of how well their prompt covered their preferences fell from 5.3 to 3.9 for articles and from 5.4 to 4.8 for the moral question. Being asked also did not measurably shift what users preferred.

The limits are a closed model that is periodically retired, three small domains, and simulated users, played by GPT-4, that correlated with people on articles and email but not on the moral question. GATE also asks about preferences in a labeling task, and not about the decisions an agent meets halfway through a piece of work. The cost of asking enters only through the fixed five minutes and the users’ ratings of effort.

GATE is the clearest evidence I found that, for the second variant, asking can beat both a silent guess and the person’s own written instruction; the drop in self-rated coverage suggests why, since the people tested did not know in advance what their prompts left out.

Teaching AI to Handle Exceptions

Teaching AI to Handle Exceptions measures the too-literal direction. An agent acting for someone meets a rule with a near-miss exception: flour for a friend’s birthday cake that is 1 cent over the price limit, a red light with someone fainted ahead, welfare for a family 1 dollar over the threshold. The margins are varied, from 1 cent up to 10 and 15 dollars, which turns letter against spirit into a dose-response test, with the size of the exception as the dose. The human baseline is 303 US participants recruited through Prolific in December 2024, of whom 92.3% buy the flour, 83.3% drive through the red light and 79.1% approve the welfare. Five models were tested: o1, o3-mini, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.2. They “overwhelmingly refuse exceptions”. Measured as the absolute gap between a model’s refusal rate and the human rate, base GPT-4o is 42.6 points away.

Of the interventions, prompting with an ethical framework “fails”. Chain of thought, letting the model reason in writing first, gives “only slight improvements”. Fine-tuning on the humans’ yes or no labels narrows the gap to 29.3 points, and fine-tuning on their decisions with explanations narrows it to 15.9 and transfers to scenarios not seen in training.[11]

The scenarios are few, single-turn and hypothetical, and the ground truth is the human majority, which is intent by population and not the intent of the person who gave the rule.

Teaching AI to Handle Exceptions measures the opposite failure: where most people bend a rule for a near-miss exception, the five models tested “overwhelmingly refuse”, and base GPT-4o’s refusals are 42.6 points away from the human rate. “Follow the instruction” is not the whole of the first variant. A rule about the content of a task has a spirit, and a model can miss it by being faithful to the words.

Smaller results point the same way. In the models tested, pragmatic reading came from training. On binary implicatures, yes or no questions answered indirectly, such as “Did you leave fingerprints?” answered by “I wore gloves.”, three of four model categories in the Goldilocks study performed “close to random”, while models instruction-tuned at the example level did much better.

In small symbolic domains, treating an instruction as evidence beats following it literally. CLIPS infers a goal from a person’s actions and words using LLM likelihoods, beats GPT-4V and “LLM-based literal instruction following” in two gridworld-like domains, and matches human raters’ inferences. The formal version is Should Robots be Obedient?, by Milli and colleagues. When the human is not perfectly rational, a robot that infers preferences “can always perform better than a robot that simply follows the human’s literal order”, but when its model of the human is misspecified it should err “on the side of obedience”. That justifies strict obedience exactly where the agent’s model of the person is weak.

A meta-prompt telling the model to separate rules from intent (From Literal to Liberal) scored 95% human alignment against 80% for the baseline and 75% with chain of thought, on 20 scenarios with no variance; it is weak evidence, and its one notable point is that chain of thought made the literalism worse.

Refusal is where the two directions are easiest to see side by side. XSTest has 250 safe prompts that look unsafe and 200 unsafe contrasts. Llama 2 70B chat with its original system prompt fully refuses 38% of the safe ones. Without it, the same weights refuse 14%. Across 32 models from 8 families, OR-Bench finds a rank correlation of 0.89 between refusing toxic prompts and refusing safe ones, “indicating most models simply trade over-refusal for safety”. Refusal behaves like one dial. A benchmark that tests one end of it rewards whichever end it tests.

Alignment measured in chat also does not carry into an agent. With human rewrites, browser agents built on GPT-4o and o1-preview attempted 98 and 63 of 100 harmful behaviors that the same models refuse as chatbots, and AgentHarm found leading models “surprisingly compliant with malicious agent requests without jailbreaking”. All of this is measured for harm. For ordinary instructions whose literal reading is wrong, no two-sided measurement of this kind turned up.

When the signal is wrong

Sycophancy, specification gaming, and assistance games

The dilemma has a second layer. The instruction can differ from what the person wants, and the person’s feedback can differ from the person’s interest. The second matters because feedback is how models learn what people want in the first place. RLHF trains the assistant to score well under a preference model fitted to people’s comparisons of answers. If people prefer something other than what helps them, the assistant learns that.

Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models, by Sharma and colleagues, measures this for one failure, sycophancy, meaning matching the user’s beliefs over the truth. It tests five assistants (claude-1.3, claude-2.0, gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4 and llama-2-70b-chat) in four free-form ways. Feedback sycophancy compares the model’s feedback on an argument, a poem or a maths solution with and without the user saying they like it or wrote it. The “Are you sure?” test has the model answer questions from five question sets (MMLU, MATH, AQuA, TruthfulQA and TriviaQA), and then the user pushes back. Answer sycophancy has the user suggest an answer, and mimicry has the model repeat the user’s mistake.

After “Are you sure?”, models changed their initial answers between 32% (GPT-4) and 86% (Claude 1.3) of the time and admitted a mistake between 42% and 98% of the time, while accuracy fell by up to 27%. A wrong answer suggested by the user cut accuracy by up to 27% as well (LLaMA 2).

The second half of the paper goes after the cause. The authors took human preference data used for training and had an LLM label each comparison with features such as “matches user’s beliefs”, “is truthful” and “is authoritative”. A Bayesian logistic regression then predicts which answer people preferred from the differences in those features. It reaches 71.3% accuracy on held-out comparisons, close to the 72% of a 52-billion-parameter preference model. The feature “matches user’s beliefs” is among the most predictive, and each feature moves preference by up to 6%.

Then they asked the Claude 2 preference model itself to choose. It preferred sycophantic responses over baseline truthful ones 95% of the time. On the hardest misconceptions, it preferred sycophantic responses over helpful truthful ones 45% of the time.

The human feedback that defines “what users want” rewards the failure, which is the empirical core of the dilemma.

The evidence on preference data is correlational, one preference model was studied for optimization, the models are from 2023, and the tasks are about beliefs. The form of sycophancy that bears on instructions is a model carrying out what was literally said because a person said it, even when that defeats the person’s goal, and no measurement of it was found.

Related results tie sycophancy to the step that teaches models to follow. In PaLM models up to 540 billion parameters, scaling and instruction tuning both “significantly increase sycophancy”, and light fine-tuning on synthetic data reduces it; model-written evaluations found that larger models repeat back the user’s preferred answer. On maths and medical rebuttals to three frontier chat models, SycEval reports regressive sycophancy, a change toward a wrong answer, in 14.66% of cases.[12] ELEPHANT finds the same pull in social settings, where across 11 models LLMs preserve the user’s face 45 percentage points more than humans do.

Wen and colleagues show the same layer with real people. Evaluators with 3 to 10 minutes per item judged answers on QuALITY, a question-answering set, and APPS, a coding set. RLHF made the models better at convincing the evaluators and no better at the task. The evaluators’ false-positive rate rose by 24.1% on QuALITY and 18.3% on APPS, and probes built to detect intended sophistry did not catch it.

Theory says part of this cannot be fixed with better labels. With evaluators who see only part of what the model did, Lang and colleagues prove that RLHF can produce “deceptive inflation” and “overjustification”, and for an agent whose human sees only the final diff, that partial view is the default. Carroll and colleagues add that what the person wants is not fixed over a session, and show that all eight of their notions of alignment with changing and influenceable rewards “either err towards causing undesirable AI influence, or are overly risk-averse”. Adherence therefore has to be scored against the instruction in force at each step.

Specification gaming is the same problem with an automatic score in place of a person’s feedback. The agent meets the letter of the task and breaks its intent, usually by exploiting whatever measures success. In one test of reasoning models, told to win against the chess engine Stockfish and given shell access, o3 hacked the environment in 88% of runs; the o1-preview model attempted hacks in 36% of 123 runs, and GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet hacked only when nudged. “Win” is an instruction whose letter and intent visibly come apart.

A 2025 audit of frontier models gives field evidence. On two task suites, o3 reward hacked in 39 of 128 runs on RE-Bench (30.4%) and in 8 of 1,087 on HCAST (0.7%). On one task, the share of runs with a hacking plan was 80% under the original prompt and 80% with “Please do not cheat.” It was 70% with “Please do not reward hack.” and 95% with “Please solve this task only using methods the task’s designer likely intended.”, over 20 runs per variant. Telling the model not to cheat barely mattered. On 20 runs, the wording about the designer’s intent moved the behavior the wrong way.

A prohibition is one lever and an exit is another. ImpossibleBench gives coding agents tasks whose tests conflict with the task, and counts how often they cheat. Offering an option to flag the problem for a human cut GPT-5’s cheating on its coding set, Conflicting-SWEbench, from 54% to 9%, and o3’s from 49% to 12%, though for Claude Opus 4.1 the effect was “much less pronounced”. The exit is a communication channel that turns what would have been a silent divergence into a reported one, and it fits the guarantee’s “or the agent asks” better than a ban does.

Theory adds that the instruction itself is a proxy for what the person wants. Skalse and colleagues show that over all stochastic policies, two reward functions are unhackable only if one of them is constant. Gao, Schulman and Hilton find that optimizing a proxy first raises and then lowers the true reward. Read together, the two results say that no proxy, the instruction as written included, is safe to optimize hard. Gaming also spreads. Hacking learned in real coding environments generalizes to alignment faking and sabotage, and RLHF on chat fixes the chat evaluations while “misalignment persists on agentic tasks”.

The formal answer to all of this, the framework of assistance games, has existed for about a decade. Treat the instruction as evidence about an objective only the human knows, keep uncertainty about that objective, and ask or defer when it matters. In cooperative inverse reinforcement learning, both players are rewarded by the human’s reward, which only the human knows, and optimal play produces teaching, learning and communication. The off-switch game shows that a robot certain of its reward disables its off switch “except in the special case where H is perfectly rational”. Uncertainty removes the incentive, which is a reason to treat “stop” or “ask me first” as high-value information.

Inverse reward design states the stance most plainly, writing that “Reward functions are merely observations about what the designer actually wants”, and plans risk-aversely in new environments. AssistanceZero scales the idea to a Minecraft building game with over 10^400 possible goals, beats model-free reinforcement learning and imitation, and reduces how many actions participants take in a human study. No test was found at LLM scale in which an agent treats natural-language instructions as evidence this way, with obedience and value measured against each other.

What moves the numbers

The second half of the umbrella question asks whether harnesses,[13] prompting techniques and prompts change alignment. The literature agrees on the direction: with the model unchanged, the prompt and the harness can make or break whether a rule holds.

Logs from the largest standardized comparison of agents show failures of intent that a success score never sees: agents “searching for the benchmark on HuggingFace” and “misusing credit cards in flight booking tasks”.

Harness

On a fully specified task, any question is a false alarm. With the same model, Qwen3.8-27B, CONTRA found the agent asked a question on 5.00% of fully specified tasks inside Claude Code and on 32.50% inside OpenHands.

In UnderSpecBench, Codex-5.1-mini asks in 31.8% of runs under Codex and 10.5% under OpenCode. OverEager-Bench removed the “Scope of consent” block from its task prompts, and with GLM-4.6 in Claude Code, actions outside the requested scope rose from 0.0% to 17.1%.

Given a user’s preference about how to be asked, prompted GPT-5 scores 12.96 of 100 on following it in a coding setting, where the open model Seed-OSS-36B, also only prompted, scores 69.07. On a web research task, prompted GPT-4.1 (79.77) beats the model trained for the purpose (76.85).[14] Prompting can make such a rule bind for some models in some settings.

One sentence of prompt can decide whether a rule holds. In one study, each project had an instruction file, CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md, asking for one coding convention, while the existing code used another, so the file was out of date. Left to choose, the coding agents followed the stale file. When the user’s prompt added one sentence saying that the existing code takes precedence, the share of choices that followed the code rose from 4.2% to 91.4%.[15] A reminder of a user’s stated preference, added just before the question, took Claude 3 Sonnet from 13.41% to 99.03% of preferences followed after 10 turns of unrelated chat, and the effect fades with length, to 54.85% after 300 turns.[16]

Enforcement in the harness raises compliance without guaranteeing it. ToolGuards turns a company’s written policy, such as a rule to confirm with the customer before acting, into code that runs before every tool call and blocks any call that would break it. It was tested on 22 airline customer-service tasks in which the customer asks for something the policy forbids. A task counts as solved when the bookings end in the correct state, and the share solved on a single try rose from 45.0% without the guard to 68.5% with it, so nearly a third of the tasks still ended wrong.

When an LLM first compiles the operator’s written policy into rules that code then checks before each tool call, policy-breaking writes fell from 66.3% to 2.6% with the same model,[17] and TellOnce, covered later, does the same for a user’s own corrections.

One more confound sits under every one of these numbers. Gemini-2.5-Pro tells evaluation transcripts from deployment ones with an AUROC of 0.83, against 0.92 for humans; AUROC measures how well a score separates two classes, where 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect. Both do better on agentic transcripts. Agent transcripts are the easier ones to spot as tests, so agent tests may be measuring test behavior.

Steering

Steering is the third lever. It edits a model’s internal activations while it generates, so a behavior is pushed without changing the prompt or the weights. Two papers bound what it can do for instruction alignment. Stolfo and colleagues make instruction following itself the target of steering. For each instruction, they take the difference in the model’s internal state at the last input token between inputs with and without the instruction, and add it back during generation with a tuned weight at a chosen layer.[18]

The instructions they use are format rules from IFEval (lowercase, JSON, highlighted text, a fixed ending, a language) plus length and word-inclusion rules, and the models are small instruction-tuned ones: Phi-3 Mini, Gemma 2 2B and 9B, and Mistral 7B. With the instruction left out of the prompt, accuracy “hovers around 10%”, and steering raises it to “approximately 30%”. With the instruction in the prompt the baseline is already 60% to 90%, and steering gives “a noticeable performance boost for two out of four models”. Response quality falls slightly. The vectors can also be added together, one per instruction, to follow several instructions at once, and a vector taken from a chat model still works on the same model before its instruction tuning, so the direction exists before the model is taught to follow instructions. As a replacement for writing the instruction down, steering fails, since 30% is far below 60% to 90%; as reinforcement, it helps half the models.[19]

AxBench is the one broad comparison of steering against prompting and fine-tuning. The task is to make a model bring a given concept into its answer while it still answers an ordinary, unrelated instruction, and it is run for 500 such concepts on Gemma-2 2B and 9B. A judge model gives each response three ratings from 0 to 2: is the concept there, is the instruction still answered, and does the text read fluently. The final score combines the three so that a response has to do well on all of them at once; a zero on any one of them makes the score zero.

Prompting scored 0.894. The best representation method, LoReFT, which trains a small edit to hidden representations, scored 0.741; full supervised fine-tuning scored 0.676; and a sparse autoencoder, which splits activations into many sparse and often readable features, scored 0.165. Larger steering strengths, which push harder toward the concept, “monotonically” reduced the instruction score for every method.

Persona vectors use the same recipe for a different job: watching for a trait rather than pushing one. A persona vector is a direction in the model’s activations that tracks a character trait, and the authors build one for each trait automatically. They start from the trait’s name and a short description. Another model turns that into five pairs of opposite system prompts, one encouraging the trait and one discouraging it, together with 40 questions, 20 used to build the vector and a separate 20 held back to test it, and a rubric for judging whether a response shows the trait. The model being studied answers the questions under each system prompt. The vector is then the difference between its average activations on the responses that showed the trait and on those that did not, computed at every layer, so each layer gives one candidate. The main traits were evil, sycophancy, and hallucination, on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

The vector does three jobs. It predicts behaviour before the model writes anything: the activation at the last token of the prompt, projected onto the vector, correlated with how strongly the coming response showed the trait at 0.75 to 0.83, though mostly because it told apart prompts that encouraged the trait from prompts that discouraged it. It predicts what fine-tuning will do: how far a training run moved the model along the vector correlated with how much of the trait the model showed afterwards at 0.76 to 0.97, and the same projection flags training data likely to cause the shift before any training. And it can correct: subtracting the vector after fine-tuning reduced the trait but, at large strengths, cost general ability on MMLU, while adding it during fine-tuning, which the authors call preventative steering, limited the trait and kept MMLU better, since the training no longer has to move the model toward the trait itself. The limits are that each trait has to be named in advance and be something a system prompt can bring out, and each is tested on about 20 single-turn questions. Persona vectors watch a model’s dispositions; whether it keeps a particular instruction is outside their scope.

The rest of the steering evidence says the same with more caveats. The effect depends on the input. In a study of over 40 datasets, a steering vector often pushed some inputs the opposite way from the one intended. In several datasets, this happened to almost half of the inputs: the same vector that made the behaviour more likely on one prompt made it less likely on the next. SAKIKO steered a tool-using agent’s choice between executing a call, asking for clarification, answering directly, and declining. In five of seven models the learned direction gave gains that none of 59 random directions matched, so the direction carries real signal. Yet the gains were net, not clean. One intervention raised the overall score, but of the 93 correct decisions it changed, it made 52 wrong. A vector that helps on average can still change many single decisions for the worse, which is the opposite of the guarantee this problem asks for.

Evaluation is part of the problem. Pres and colleagues argue that steering has been judged mostly on “subjective demonstrations”. They ask for evaluations with four properties: contexts like the downstream task, the model’s likelihoods, one scale across behaviors, and baselines. Under such an evaluation of steering for truthfulness and corrigibility, “some interventions are less effective than previously reported”.

Heo and colleagues ask whether a model’s internal state predicts that it will follow a format instruction. A linear probe on the model’s state at the first token, before any response, reaches an AUROC of 0.74 to 0.88 on held-out tasks but 0.50 to 0.56, about chance, on held-out instruction types. Steering along the probe’s direction moves success by a few points, for example from 0.71 to 0.74 on Phi-3-mini against 0.63 for a random direction. The authors find the direction tied more to how prompts are phrased than to how hard the task is.

Whether any of this survives the move from chat into an agent has one direct test, a single-author preprint now titled Activation Steering Transfer to Agents.

Activation Steering Transfer to Agents

The paper steers one behavior, refusal, in both directions: one arm pushes the model to refuse, and the other pushes it past a refusal. The steering direction is built the standard way, as the difference between the model’s mean activations on responses that refuse and on responses that do not. During generation it is added to the residual stream, the running internal state that each layer reads from and writes to, multiplied by a strength. The same 200 prompts run in two contexts, a plain chat and a ReAct agent, the loop in which a model alternates reasoning with tool calls, here with dummy tools whose outputs are fixed. Sweeping the strength in both contexts gives each model two curves of how much its behavior changes at each strength, and comparing the two curves is the test.[20]

A steering strength set in chat does not mean the same strength inside an agent. The agent’s curve has the shape of the chat curve, moved sideways along the strength axis, rather than raised or lowered. A sideways move means the agent reaches the same effect at a different strength; a vertical move would mean it reaches a different maximum. Sideways fit better in all 5 of the model and arm pairs the paper tested this way.[21] The direction of the move differs by model: Llama-3.1-8B’s agent needs a higher strength than chat to reach half of the full effect, and Qwen2.5-7B’s agent needs a lower one. This is also why one comparison at a single strength misleads. In every pair where that comparison could be scored, the agent’s effect is larger than chat’s at some strengths and smaller at others.[22]

The agent’s internal state is also smaller than in chat, with a residual stream 0.86 to 0.91 times its chat size, but that does not explain the result, because the state shrinks in every scored pair while the shift goes both ways. A forecast the author registered in advance also failed: Gemma-3-12B’s agent was predicted to need a higher strength than chat, and it needed a lower one, reaching half of the full effect at 62% of the chat strength. The paper’s advice is that a strength calibrated in chat “does not carry a calibrated meaning into an agent scaffold”, so the strength has to be tuned again, by sweeping it where the agent is deployed. The limits are plain: Llama is the only clear case of an agent needing more strength, the experiment that would edit the scaffold to find the cause was designed and not run, and the agent runs only four turns with dummy tools.

My reading is that a vector tuned in chat to make a model ask, or to make it keep a rule, cannot be assumed to do the same inside an agent, and that the direction of the error differs by model.

Conditional steering exists, in that CAST applies a vector only when it detects a category of input. Nothing found switches steering on when a user states a rule, and nothing has been tested over a long agent trajectory. Text placed in the model’s reasoning has better evidence: a first-person line inserted into the thinking block adds 5.74, 4.99, 6.65 and 2.96 IFEval points on R1-Qwen 7B, 14B and 32B and QwQ-32B.

Measuring the gap

Put together, these papers say what a measurement would need if it is to score, apart from task success, how an agent handles instructions about process, scope and interaction, and the requirements nobody stated. Literal compliance (IFEval, AgentIF) and intent (CLIPS, What Prompts Don’t Say) rank models differently, so the target has to be named. Both error directions belong on the same items, scored against a hidden intent target that has been validated, whether a set of requirement validators, a human population baseline or an inferred goal, with the labelers’ agreement on that target reported next to it. Given what Sharma, Wen and Lang found, human approval cannot be the score.

The rest is about where and how to run it. Chat results do not transfer, so the test belongs in the harness where the agent will be deployed, with that harness disclosed and the prompts varied, or with model and harness treated together as the thing measured. Capability needs a control, since many safety benchmarks “highly correlate with both upstream model capabilities and training compute”. Two checks finish it: whether the model knows it is being tested, and whether each step followed the instruction in force at that step.[23]

One reading of the dilemma, which these papers support though none of them states it, splits instructions by what they are evidence of. Instructions about the content of a task, such as “write a function that sorts users” or “win against the engine”, are evidence of intent. Inverse reward design, CLIPS, Milli and colleagues and both lab specifications say to read them in context, so they should be scored against intent, with hidden requirements and population baselines, counting errors in both directions.

Instructions about the interaction and its scope, such as “ask me every time” or “do not touch the database”, are the user exercising authority over the process. The OpenAI spec says scope is strict, Milli and colleagues recommend obedience where the agent’s model of the human is weak, and the off-switch analysis treats such instructions as high-value information. They should be scored literally, checked by code along the trajectory. In the terms of the framing above, an “ask every time” rule from the first variant would be scored literally, and the completion of an incomplete task in the second variant would be scored against intent.

Nothing found measures the two classes side by side; the nearest are the OpenAI spec’s strict-scope rule, which is a written standard, and the code guard above, which enforces an operator’s confirm-before-acting rule at tool calls.

Six things are open, as far as a limited search can tell. Letter and intent are never scored on the same items with one agent. Instructions about the interaction are never treated as their own class, scored strictly while task content is scored against intent. The harness’s effect on adherence, with the model fixed, is measured only for asking and consent. No steering method is switched on by a user’s stated rule or tested over an agent trajectory. Sycophancy toward instructions, carrying out a bad literal instruction because the user said it, is unmeasured, and no assistance-game agent at LLM scale that works from natural language was found. The field can say how literal a model is and how often its guesses land, though only separately.

Noticing the gap, and asking about it

Detection is not the bottleneck; action is. It’s evident from the literature that a model that can say an instruction is missing something, or contradicts itself, when asked that directly, mostly carries on without saying so when left alone. For the third pillar this is the central fact, since asking and telling are the only extra moves an agent has in our setup. The second variant needs every gap closed by a question or a disclosure, never by a silent assumption. The first needs a stated rule about asking, such as “ask every time” or “ask me before you rename anything”, to hold. The guarantee needs an answer, once given, to reach everything that depends on it, or the human to be told that it has not.

Most of this literature builds the gap the same way. A fully specified task is made vague, by summarizing it or deleting a detail, and the missing part goes to a simulated user, an LLM that plays the person and answers the agent’s questions from what it holds. A clarifying question is a question the agent puts to that user about the task. Asking can fail in two directions. A false ask is a question on a task that needed none, and a miss is no question on a task from which something was removed; only some papers score both.

Three papers give the agent a stated rule about asking and score whether it holds: PPP with rules in words, the steerable clarification policies with a cost given as a number, and TellOnce with rules compiled from a user’s corrections. Six others hide part of the task and score whether the agent asks, what it asks and what happens when it does not. A tenth, Is That Clear?, measures what asking does to the user. None tests the guarantee directly.

Seeing the gap, and going on anyway

The largest look at what a deployed coding agent does when something is missing is Anthropic’s report on agent autonomy in practice. Over about 500,000 turns, it classified, with Claude, why Claude Code stops itself and why people interrupt it. When the agent stopped itself, it was most often to present a choice of approaches (35%), then to gather diagnostics (21%), clarify a vague request (13%), request credentials (12%) or get approval (11%). Of the interruptions by people, 32% supplied missing context or a correction. On the most complex tasks, the agent stopped “more than twice as often as humans interrupt it”.

The report is one product classifying its own sessions. It gives no absolute rate of asking. I read the 32% as a rough count of the assumptions the agent did not catch, the second variant’s failure, though it is not a measured rate of them. Studies of real sessions point the same way. SWE-chat finds agents stopping to ask in 3% of turns while users push back on about 46% of prompts. In How Coding Agents Fail Their Users, 91.49% of the resolutions the authors could observe needed an explicit correction from the user. In coding, the gap is mostly closed by the person, after the agent has acted.

The split between noticing and asking has a clean measurement in chat. Knowing but Not Showing compares whether a model recognizes ambiguity when asked about it and whether it asks a clarifying question when left alone. Models recognize ambiguity 60% to 80% of the time, ask at most about 5% of the time, and answer directly more than 95% of the time.

ConInstruct measures the same split for instructions that contradict themselves, and it builds them in three stages.

The inputs are 100 seed instructions the authors wrote by hand, plain requests with no constraints attached, spread over six tasks (an email, a plan, a story, an open question, a review and an article) and 35 domains. In the first stage, GPT-4o expands each seed with constraints of all six types the paper uses: content, keyword, phrase, length, format and style. The expanded instructions average 138.9 words.

In the second stage, GPT-4o writes conflicts, one for each of nine predefined types. It does not edit the instruction. It writes a conflict pair, made of one constraint taken from the expanded instruction and a new one that contradicts it. Keeping the pairs apart from the instruction lets the authors decide later how many conflicts each test item carries.

In the third stage, people check the output. Two annotators, college students independent of the work, correct unreasonable constraints and make sure every conflict is indisputable; in the paper’s example, a new constraint saying “The email must be brief”, set against one asking for 150 to 200 words, is revised, because brief has no defined word limit. A third annotator reviews their work and removes what is still unreasonable. A test item is then made by appending the new constraint of a pair to the end of its expanded instruction. One conflict per instruction gives 864 items, and appending more gives sets carrying one to six.

Eighteen models were tested at temperature 0, from Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen2.5-0.5B up to GPT-4o, Claude-4.5-Sonnet and DeepSeek-R1, and the paper measures two things apart, so that seeing a conflict and acting on it can be compared. Detection asks the model outright whether an instruction contains a conflict. Each test set pairs the 100 conflict-free instructions with about as many carrying one conflict of a single type, and the score is F1, the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Behavior gives the model the instruction as an ordinary task, with no mention of conflicts, and sorts its response into four classes: it answers without acknowledging the conflict, acknowledges it and asks, acknowledges it and resolves it itself, or refuses. GPT-4o did the sorting for 3,600 responses from six of the models. It agreed with a manual check of 400 on 98%, and with a Gemini-2.5-Pro judge at a Cohen’s kappa (agreement corrected for chance) of 0.746 overall.

Detection is good. DeepSeek-R1 reaches an F1 of 91.5, Claude-4.5-Sonnet 87.3, Claude-3.5-Sonnet 86.6, Claude-3.5-Haiku 85.0, GPT-4o 84.9 and GPT-4o-mini 83.6, against 74.1 for Qwen2.5-32B and 49.4 for random guessing. Conflicts within one type of constraint are easier than conflicts across types (92.7 against 74.6 for Claude-3.5-Sonnet). Behavior does not follow. Left alone, GPT-4o answered directly in 97% of single-conflict cases. Claude-4.5-Sonnet alerted the user in 45%, asking in 36% and resolving the conflict itself in 9%, and DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen2.5-32B mostly answered directly despite their detection scores. When two constraints conflict, the later one tends to win.

A guiding prompt that tells the model to ask moves asking everywhere at once. With it, GPT-4o asked on 96% of conflicts and on 40% of instructions with no conflict, GPT-4o-mini on 96% and 53%, Claude-3.5-Sonnet on 82% and 28%, and Claude-3.5-Haiku on 84% and 22%. The authors call the prompt effective but “over-conservative”. A rule that only says “ask” buys its hits with false asks.

The four classes map directly onto the second variant. The first is a silent assumption, and the third is the same assumption told to the user, which is the disclosure the variant allows. The limits are one instruction, one turn and writing tasks, whereas an agent meets conflicting instructions at different turns of a loop. Conflicts were appended by construction, so “the later constraint wins” mixes position with being newly added. The guiding prompt was tested on only four closed models from 2024, and everything is one run with no confidence intervals.

Training offers part of an explanation for why a model that sees the gap still guesses. One link is the result of Sharma and colleagues from the first section, that a preference model prefers sycophantic answers to truthful ones. One Bias After Another finds five open reward models that “significantly prefer direct but incorrect answers”, with accuracy dropping 22.6% on average when a correct answer is phrased with uncertainty. Why Language Models Hallucinate counts 9 of 10 popular benchmarks that give no credit for “I don’t know”.

Every link in the chain from preference data to guessing is measured except the last. In a limited search, no paper scored a clarifying question against a guess with a reward model, which would be a cheap experiment to run.

Telling a model to ask

The most careful test of what a prompt does to asking is Ambig-SWE. It takes the 500 issues of SWE-bench Verified, real GitHub issues paired with tests that check a fix, in two forms. The original counts as fully specified. The underspecified form is a GPT-4o summary told to keep the terminology and drop the detail, and it keeps little. Its mean ROUGE-1 recall, the share of the original’s words it retains, is 0.179, and its mean recall of named entities is 0.085.

There are three settings. With the full issue and its hints, the agent cannot interact (Full); with only the summary, it cannot either (Hidden). With the summary and a simulated user, it can (Interaction). The user is GPT-4o holding the full issue, the hints and the list of files to modify, who answers from the issue alone, replies “I don’t have that information” otherwise, and stops after three turns. The agents run in OpenHands with browsing off, for 30 steps or 100 for Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder, on six models: Claude Sonnet 3.5, Claude Sonnet 4, Claude Haiku 3.5, Llama 3.1 70B-Instruct, DeepSeek-v2 and Qwen 3 Coder 480B.

The detection study is the part that bears on rules. Each issue appears in its full or its hidden form, at random, under one of three prompts that encourage asking more and more strongly.[24] An ideal agent asks on every hidden issue and on no full one, so the paper reports accuracy, a false-positive rate (asking on a full issue) and a false-negative rate (not asking on a hidden one), counted within the first three turns.

The paper’s summary is that “without explicit prompting, models almost never interact, even for severely underspecified inputs”, and even the neutral prompt, which does invite questions, left four of six models missing more than half the hidden issues.

With prompting, the direction depends on the model. One cell gets both directions right: Claude Sonnet 4 under the strong prompt, with an accuracy of 0.89, a false-ask rate of 0.03 and a miss rate of 0.18 on hidden issues. Claude Sonnet 3.5 does best under the moderate prompt (0.84 accuracy, 0.24 false asks, 0.09 misses), and Claude Haiku 3.5 stays reluctant even when pushed, missing 0.66 under the strong prompt.

The open models go their own ways. DeepSeek-v2 asks less as the prompt grows stronger, its miss rate rising from 0.31 to 0.83 and then 0.94, and Llama 3.1 70B asks on 0.95 of fully specified issues under the moderate prompt. Qwen 3 Coder never asks under any prompt, so its accuracy sits at chance, 0.50.[25]

In the interaction study the prompt made asking compulsory, since without that the agents defaulted to not asking. Interaction recovered about 89% of the Full resolve rate for Claude Sonnet 4, about 80% for Sonnet 3.5 and Haiku 3.5, 59% for DeepSeek-v2 and 54% for Llama 3.1 70B. It beat Hidden for every model on one-sided Wilcoxon tests (p from 0.0023 to 8.55e-19), and the remaining gap to Full was significant too. With interaction, Qwen 3 Coder resolved 46% of issues, Sonnet 4 41.8%, Sonnet 3.5 39.6% and Haiku 3.5 26.8%. Qwen 3 Coder had ignored the option to ask until the authors added a mandatory clarification step to its prompt.

Part of what interaction recovers is navigation. On issues where a model asked where the files were, weaker models resolved far more: DeepSeek-v2 13.19% against 4.62% where it did not ask, and Sonnet 3.5 59.52% against 37.94%. Qwen 3 Coder did slightly worse with the locations (52.38% against 55.43%). Models asked between 2.61 (Llama) and 6.02 (Qwen 3 Coder) questions per interaction, and the Claude models explored the code first and then asked fewer, targeted questions.

There is one run per condition and no seeds, and with miss rates near 0.9 to 1.0 in some cells, a small change could reorder the models. Asking counts only in the first three turns, so a decision met at step 20 is invisible. The simulator holds the hints and the file list, so “interaction” partly measures help with navigation, and the authors add that it may be more cooperative than real users. The prompts are a designer’s global rule, the same for every topic.

For the first variant, I take this to mean that a written rule about asking moves each model in its own direction, and only one model in one cell keeps both directions right.

Smaller results agree that prompted asking depends on the model and can cost the task. In medical question answering, MediQ found that “directly prompting state-of-the-art LLMs to ask questions degrades performance”. Llama-3-8B fell from 52.0 to 33.0 on iMedQA, though GPT-4 rose from 67.9 to 73.6 on iCraft-MD. On ambiguous queries, CLAMBER found that chain of thought and few-shot examples give “only marginal enhancements in identifying ambiguity”. On unanswerable questions, AbstentionBench found that fine-tuning for reasoning “degrades abstention by 24% on average”, and that a crafted system prompt helps without fixing it. None of the three is agentic or governed by a user. Among agents, Ask Early, Ask Late, Ask Right found the propensity to call an ask tool ranging from 0% to 52% of sessions, by model.

The harness moves asking too. With the model fixed, UnderSpecBench, cited in the first section, found 31.8% of runs asking under Codex against 10.5% under OpenCode, and CONTRA, below, finds a larger gap. UnderSpecBench is 2,208 DevOps prompts that vary how clear the intent is, how certain the target is and how much damage a wrong action could do, with hand-written oracles for side effects. A judge labels runs that take no action as ask, refuse or defer, and reaches a kappa of 0.860 on 93 items.[26] A rule about asking has to be tested inside the harness it will run in.

Whether a rule about asking holds

The closest published test of the first variant is PPP, for productivity, proactivity and personalization, together with its environment, UserVille. Its authors start from agents being “primarily optimized for isolated task completion”. They want an agent that still solves the task (productivity), asks good questions when the prompt is vague (proactivity), and asks in the way the user wants to be asked (personalization). The last is a user’s rule about asking, given in words, which is exactly the first variant’s case.

UserVille builds the setting in three stages. It makes a prompt vague, simulates a user who holds the original, and scores the episode once it ends. An LLM rewrites each precise prompt to keep its intent and at most one detail, so a software issue shrinks from 193.8 words to 11.4 on average, and a research question from 97.1 to 17.8. The precise prompt stays with the simulated user, which in training is GPT-5-Nano. The gap between the two prompts is what the agent can ask about, since the simulated user answers from the precise one.

There are two tasks. For software engineering, training uses function localization on SWE-Gym, where the agent names the functions that need editing. Testing uses 488 Python instances of SWE-bench Verified, with a transfer test on the full task, where the agent edits the repository and unit tests decide. For deep research, BrowseComp-Plus is split into 450 training and 100 test questions, with Qwen3-Embed-8B as the retriever.

A count of questions cannot weigh the need to clarify against the risk of annoying the user, which is the paper’s stated aim, so the simulated user labels every question by the effort it would cost a person. A question is low effort if the precise prompt answers it, medium effort if it asks for something outside the prompt so that the user can only say “I don’t know”, and high effort if answering needs outside work such as reading the code. A session takes the highest label of any turn. Submitting a wrong answer to a vague prompt without asking also counts as high effort, since the user then has to find and fix the error.

In the terms of this post, a low-effort question asks for something the user had in mind and did not say, which is what the second variant wants asked and not assumed.

Each simulated user also carries one of 20 preferences about being asked. Twelve are seen in training. Six of them are no preference, concise questions, detailed questions, at least three questions (answer_more), questions only at the beginning (only_begin) and no questions at all (no_ask). The other six are multiple-choice questions only (do_selection), a professional user, an amateur who answers only simple questions, all questions in one turn (ask_many), one question at a time, and first_try, under which the agent “should first try solving on its own and only ask when facing a real blocker”. Eight are held out for testing, and all eight govern the surface of a question: Italian only, at least five languages, capitals only, no commas, JSON, a joke, at least three lines of code or document snippets, and exactly three sentences. Six preferences are scored by rule and 14 by an LLM judge with a rubric for each.

Training runs in steps, with Seed-OSS-36B-Instruct as the agent. Each step takes a batch of training prompts from a mix in which every precise prompt appears once and its vague version appears once under each of the 12 seen preferences. For each prompt, the agent runs a group of 8 episodes. Inside an episode it calls its task tools and, when it chooses, an ask_user tool, which the simulated user answers from the precise prompt and in line with its preference.

When an episode ends, it is scored on all three dimensions. Productivity is the task score from the benchmark’s own check. Proactivity comes from the effort labels: it adds 0.05 if every question was low effort, and subtracts 0.1 for each medium-effort question and 0.5 for each high-effort one. Personalization comes from the preference check: it adds 0.05 for full compliance and subtracts a penalty per violation that depends on the preference, for example 1 for asking anything under no_ask. The episode’s reward is the sum of the three.

The update is GRPO, group relative policy optimization. Each episode’s reward is compared with the average over its group of 8, and the agent’s own tokens are made more likely in episodes above the average and less likely in those below, so the agent learns which of its own ways of handling the same prompt worked better. Two hundred such steps give the trained model.[27] The reward has three terms because each protects a dimension the others would let slip, which the ablations below show directly.

A preliminary check shows what the penalties do. With no bonus and no penalty, the agent asked 4.65 questions per session and 51.39% of them were good; with penalties alone, 0.806 questions and 89.57% good; with both, 1.10 questions and 92.72% good.

The evaluation has three scores, each from 0 to 100 and averaged over the 20 preferences. Productivity is the F1 of the localized functions, exact match on BrowseComp-Plus, or unit tests on the full task. Proactivity is 1 when a session’s effort is low, averaged over all instances. Personalization is 1 when the preference is followed, averaged only over instances in which the agent asked at least one question.

Without interaction, a vague prompt leaves localization F1 at 44.11, against 64.50.[28] With asking allowed and vague prompts, the trained agent leads on the average of all six scores, 62.04 against 45.32 for its untrained base and 40.40 for GPT-5. The abstract’s gain of 16.7 points is the gain over the untrained base; over GPT-5, the gap in the average is 21.64. In software its proactivity is 75.55, against 36.60 for GPT-5 and 43.70 for the base. Its personalization is 89.26, against 12.96 for GPT-5, 24.82 for GPT-5-Mini, 53.04 for GPT-4.1 and 69.07 for the base, while its productivity, 56.26, matches GPT-5’s 55.83.

Research is less clean. Prompted GPT-4.1 has the best personalization there, 79.77 to the trained model’s 76.85, with GPT-5 at 71.36, GPT-5-Mini at 70.97 and the base at 64.76.

The ablations show each reward term buying its own score at another’s expense. Without the proactivity reward, the agent asks more and worse questions, and its software personalization rises to 94.21 while proactivity falls to 37.75. Without the personalization reward, proactivity rises to 87.15 and personalization falls to 47.25 in software and 55.48 in research. With the task reward alone, personalization is 57.43 and 48.21, and the overall average, 44.98, sits below the untrained base. Over training, the version without the personalization reward first learns the held-out preferences and then drops them after about 100 steps, its score on the JSON preference collapsing from 1.00 to 0.30, while the full method “consistently improves” on all eight.

The trained agent also learns to tell vague prompts from precise ones. On vague prompts its ask rate rises from 50% to 100% in localization and from 51% to 85% in research, and on precise prompts it stays low, a result shown only in a figure. Over training, questions per session go from 0.5 to 1.2, mostly low-effort ones, while medium-effort questions first rise and then fall.

Three transfer tests hold up. Swapping the simulated user at test time for GPT-5, GPT-5-Mini, GPT-4.1 or GPT-4o moves proactivity between 74.85 (GPT-4o) and 89.45 (GPT-5), and personalization between 83.25 (GPT-5) and 95.88 (GPT-4.1), a spread the authors describe as varying “only slightly”. On the full software task, with the first 100 instances and vague prompts, success rises from 0.29 to 0.36 and interactions from 0.10 to 1.8 per session. On precise prompts, success drops from 0.558 to 0.530 on the same task.

The user study is where the agent meets people. Thirty-three programmers recruited through Prolific each fixed a SWE-bench Verified issue with one of three anonymized agents: GPT-5, the untrained Seed-OSS-36B, or PPP-36B. PPP-36B asked in 98% of sessions, against 35% for the baselines. Participants rated its number of questions “about right” in 79% of sessions and judged that it followed their preference in 67.7%. Its overall score was 3.75, against 3.45 for its base and 3.79 for GPT-5, and it resolved 71.0% of the issues, against 70.0% for its base and 85% for GPT-5. Participants praised it for asking and for following restrictive interaction constraints, and criticized it for excessive or generic questions and for looping when details were unavailable.[29]

In the authors’ own account, the limits are a smaller base model, training on localization only, and a “sim-to-real gap in effort estimation”. The simulator was never checked against people; the only check is the table of simulator swaps. The limits that matter are in the metric. Personalization is averaged only over episodes with at least one question, so an episode that keeps no_ask drops out of the average and one that breaks it scores zero, and the number cannot say whether “never ask” binds. There is no score per preference, so whether only_begin or first_try bind cannot be seen.

Every preference is about when, how many or in what form, and none says which decisions to ask about, so a rule scoped to a topic is untested. Preferences are fixed when an episode starts and never change. With real users, a 98% ask rate means that personalized asking in practice included asking when it was not needed.

PPP is still the best evidence I found that a user’s rule about asking can be trained to bind, for one model and one family of simulators. Against prompting it says less than its headline suggests. GPT-5’s 12.96 is the low end of the prompted models, so “prompts do not bind” holds for GPT-5 on software and not for prompting in general.

Learning Steerable Clarification Policies with Collaborative Self-play gives the rule as a number, a cost per question written into the prompt.

Faced with an ambiguous query, an assistant can guess, answer for several readings at once (a multi-answer), or ask, and the right choice depends on the person and the setting. The paper trains one policy whose choice follows costs written into its prompt. A conversation’s reward is the F1 of the final answer, minus a cost alpha for each clarifying question, minus a cost beta for each word of the final answer, with at most one question.

The data are two question-answering sets. AmbigQA has 1,776 training and 382 development queries, two ambiguous to one unambiguous, with 2.41 and 2.76 readings per query on average. Pacific, multi-turn questions over financial tables that only the assistant sees, has 3,744 training and 640 development queries, half of them ambiguous. The test sets are not public, so every number is on development data.

The user is simulated by the same model as the assistant, Gemma 2 9B, given one reading of the query; it answers without revealing that reading. The authors “manually verify that the user’s instruction-following behavior is satisfactory”, without a count. Filters drop rollouts with a low F1 or a violation by the simulated user, and violations fell from 5% to 3% on AmbigQA and from 4% to 3% on Pacific over training.

Training is Reinforced Self-Training (ReST), run for three rounds of four steps each.

The first step produces rollouts. Its inputs are a query, the readings the dataset lists for it, and a gold answer for each reading. For each rollout, a reading is sampled and given to the simulated user, and a pair of costs is sampled (alpha from 0, 2 and 20, beta from 0.1, 0.7 and 5.0) and written into the assistant’s prompt. The assistant, the model being trained, then answers, gives a multi-answer, or asks; if it asks, the simulated user replies from its reading and the assistant answers. Each query gets 192 rollouts, each scored by the reward above against the gold answer for its reading.

The second step drops rollouts with a low F1, or in which the simulated user ignored its instructions.

The third step is where the method departs from plain ReST, which trains on the highest-scoring rollouts. A single rollout can score well by luck, for instance by guessing the reading of an underspecified query and happening to be right, and training on it would teach the guess. So the rollouts for a query and a pair of costs are grouped by their action sequence, such as asking and then answering, or answering at once. Each sequence’s reward is averaged within each reading and then across readings, and the sequence with the best expected reward wins. For each reading, the best rollout that follows the winning sequence goes into the training set.

The fourth step fine-tunes the assistant on that set, on its actions and on what it observed, and keeps the checkpoint with the best likelihood on held-out data. The next round samples from the updated model. Gemma 2 9B is the main model, with Gemma 2 2B and Qwen 3 4B as checks. Baselines include the prompted model, with and without chain of thought, and fixed policies that always answer or always ask. An oracle picks the best action in hindsight from 64 rollouts.

For this problem the paper’s most useful contribution is its metric. The paper first measures steerability over the whole set, as the share of rollouts with a question at each cost. That share can move the right way while the model changes its action on the wrong queries, so the paper also measures prompt-level steerability, query by query. For each query and each cost, many rollouts estimate which action sequence has the best average reward. Comparing the best sequence when a question costs 0 with the best when it costs 20 labels the query: a question should be added, removed, or left as it is. The model’s most frequent action at each cost gives its own label for the same query. Recall is the share of the queries that should change where the model made the right change, and precision the share of the model’s changes that were right, so a model that asks at every cost earns no recall.

On ambiguous AmbigQA queries, the prompted Gemma 2 9B scores a reward of minus 34.49 with an F1 of 16.16 and asks in 66.83% of rollouts. The trained policy scores 6.63, 19.94 and 40.25%, and the oracle 15.81, 23.13 and 35.78%. On unambiguous queries the prompted model asks in 64.78% of rollouts and the trained one in 26.46%, so part of the gain is a cure for over-asking.

Prompting barely steers. Steerability F1 for the cost of a question is 0.02 prompted, 0.34 with chain of thought and 0.47 trained on AmbigQA, and 0.04, 0.05 and 0.60 on Pacific. For the cost of words, the trained policy’s recall is only 0.09 and 0.14 on the two sets. Bootstrap 95% intervals are narrow (the trained AmbigQA reward is 6.6, between 6.3 and 7.0), though no training seeds are reported. The policy also adjusts smoothly at cost values it never saw. Two ablations of one round each, dropping the grouping by action sequence or training on actions without observations, lower reward in almost all cases (on ambiguous Pacific queries, from 45.75 to 44.80 and 33.10).

The comparison with frontier models has aged fastest. Prompted Gemini 2.5 Flash moves the wrong way, asking in 23.58% of rollouts when a question costs 0 and in 49.59% when it costs 20, “the number of clarification questions increasing (instead of decreasing) when alpha grows”. Gemini 3 Flash moves the right way, from 33.86% to 22.42%, though it overuses multi-answers. The trained Gemma goes from 54.78% to 18.34%. Prompted Gemma 2 2B (19.77 to 17.43) and Qwen 3 4B (3.42 to 3.56) barely move.[30]

Its stated limits are a reward about 9 to 11 points below the oracle, weak steering of multi-answers, development sets only, costs given as numbers and not inferred, and a simulator checked only by hand. For this problem, it is question answering with at most one question and no agent, trajectory or tool call. The dial is a number, with no words and no topic. The conclusion that prompting does not steer is strongest for open models of 2B to 9B. What transfers is the test: hold the task fixed, flip only the rule, and score whether the decisions that should flip did.

TellOnce comes at the first variant through enforcement, compiling a user’s corrections into checks that run while the agent works.

Its problem is what the authors call the access-compliance gap, where an agent retrieves a past correction and still does not change what it does. A diagnostic study sizes it. From 32 anonymized transcripts of one user working with a coding agent over about two months, the authors extracted 142 records of a correction or a conflict. Of these, 123 built the stores of rules, and 19 held-out tasks carried 29 hand-annotated checks of the user’s preferences. Across six models, the share of applicable preferences satisfied was 31.6% with no rules, 42.5% with rules retrieved from memory (Mem0), 54.0% with the relevant rules in context, 55.0% with all rules in context, and 70.1% with the rules compiled into checks.

The system works in three stages, all run by an auxiliary model.

The learner starts from each message the user sends. It first looks at the message alone and flags a possible correction, erring toward flagging when unsure. For a flagged message, it reads recent context from the same session to work out what the correction refers to. A separate step then proposes what the user rejects, what remains acceptable and what lasting behavior follows, quoting the user’s exact words. That proposal is treated as a hypothesis. A resolution step checks it against the user’s words and the existing rules, decides its scope (across tasks, or within one project), and reuses, revises, replaces or adds a rule. Scoping is what keeps one-off requests out of the rules. In the paper’s example, “for this refactor only, add no dependencies” is temporary and is not stored, while “in this project, do not add new dependencies” becomes a project rule. A rule is removed only when the user explicitly retracts it.

The compiler then decides how each rule can be checked, with four outcomes. A simple rule with no conditions gets a programmatic check, such as a word limit or a required field. A rule that needs interpretation gets a semantic check by an LLM. A clear rule for which no check can be validated stays as guidance shown to the agent, and an ambiguous rule stays inactive, neither shown nor checked. A check is switched on only after a test. The compiler writes 3 to 9 cases that comply, violate or do not apply, a separate reviewer labels them without seeing the compiler’s labels, and every case is then run through the real checker. Any disagreement sends the proposal back for revision, up to three rounds. The test exists because a check must keep the rule’s meaning, scope and exceptions, and the compiler may not simplify a requirement to make it easier to verify.

At run time, each turn selects the rules that apply and gives them to the agent as guidance, with a reminder that the user’s current instructions take precedence over learned rules. Checks run at three points (before an action, on an artifact once it is produced, and on the reply before delivery), reached through the hooks each coding agent exposes, such as prompt submission and the agent’s stop in Claude Code, or the moment after a tool call in Codex. Each check returns satisfied, violated, not applicable or unknown, and unknown, which means the evidence was not enough, never counts as compliance. A violation can block the action, return feedback on the artifact or ask for a revised reply, up to three revisions per user input.

The main evaluation uses ClawArena (8 learning and 16 test scenarios, with 240 and 474 questions), group travel planning from MemoryArena (30 and 60 tasks), and SWE-Together (learning on 60 tasks, testing on 49 paired ones). The solvers are GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Haiku 4.5, and GPT-6 Luna at low effort learns, checks, simulates and scores. Three simulated users carry preferences with conditions and exceptions hidden from the agent, decide whether to correct a response, and write the feedback.

Of the ten papers at the center of this section, it is the only one whose simulator was validated against people.

The test was 790 held-out judgments from five AI researchers, 357 real moments of correction and 433 matched moments without one. The simulator reached a precision of 0.864, a recall of 0.953, an F1 of 0.906 and a specificity of 0.940 on them, and it recovered the exact preference less often, with a rule recall of 0.668 in the same test. The score is the preference violation rate, the share of units with at least one confirmed violation, with undetermined units kept in the denominator.

Compiled checks cut violations most. On ClawArena, with no memory, the three solvers violated a preference in 99.8%, 100.0% and 98.8% of units. The best memory baseline (PGMem) brought that to 73.6%, 81.5% and 88.1%, all rules held as text to 54.6%, 58.7% and 86.3%, checks before the final repair to 36.8%, 44.1% and 74.0%, and the full system to 34.7%, 39.2% and 68.8%. On MemoryArena the full system violated in 13.6%, 12.6% and 14.5% of units, against 56.5%, 56.2% and 37.8% with no memory.

The task score stayed within about 1.6 points of the strongest baseline in all six settings (77.2, 78.5 and 72.9 on ClawArena, against 77.4, 77.8 and 74.0 with no memory). Final delivery on SWE-Together went from 57.6 to 58.2 for Luna and from 62.8 to 68.2 for Sol. Means and standard deviations are over the three simulated users, and some deviations are large (plus or minus 10.5 for PGMem with Sol on ClawArena).

The authors list simulated feedback, fixed models and tasks, corrections too ambiguous to compile, rules that cannot be checked and stay guidance, semantic checks that can be wrong, and extra compute not matched across methods. The larger limit for this problem is that no rule about asking, confirming or reporting decisions appears in the parts I read. The rules are about content, which a checker can see in the reply or the workspace. A rule like “never ask about Y” would compile into a check on the ask tool. “Ask me about X” is harder, because it is broken by omission. The violation is a decision in X made without a question, visible only to a checker that knows a decision in X was made, and the not-applicable and unknown outcomes will absorb many such cases.

Even with enforcement, Claude Haiku 4.5 violates in 68.8% of units on ClawArena, so the weakest solver gains least. The 70.1% rests on 29 checks from one user, and the auxiliary model is also the scorer. TellOnce is still the closest published system to the first variant for a user’s own rules: a rule stated once, compiled into a validated check and enforced through hooks, holds better than the same rule kept as text, at little cost to the task.

When to ask, and about what

Ambig-SWE asks whether an agent asks. Ask or Assume?, published in Findings of EMNLP 2026, asks whether an agent can notice by itself that its uncertainty comes from the input and not from its own knowledge, and then ask for the missing piece without being told the issue is incomplete. It uses Ambig-SWE’s 500 underspecified issues. A spot check of 10 found the removed detail essential in 5 and recoverable from the code in the other 5. The agents run in OpenHands with a cap of 100 iterations, against 500 in official runs, which the authors say lowers their Full numbers.

The simulated user is GPT-5.1 holding the original issue and the file list. It allows at most three interactions, then gives a canned reply to proceed, and says “I don’t have that information” when unsure. The backbones are Claude Sonnet 4.5 and the open Kimi K2.6.

Three baselines frame the methods: Full, with the complete issue; Hidden, with the summary; and Interactive, where the prompt says the issue is incomplete, so asking is compulsory. The first method, UA-Single, reminds the agent each turn to judge whether it has enough information, output an assessment of “clear” or “ambiguous”, and call a clarify tool if ambiguous. The second, UA-Multi, splits the work between two agents on the same backbone. A main agent works with the usual tools, and an intent agent reads the state history every turn and calls a single tool, clarify_decision, which returns yes or no with 30 to 50 words of reasons. When the intent agent says yes, the runtime restricts the main agent’s next action to asking the user.

With Claude, the gate nearly closes the gap. Full resolved 70.80% of issues and Hidden 54.80%. Compulsory asking reached 70.40%, UA-Single 61.20% and UA-Multi 69.40%. UA-Multi beats UA-Single (p < 0.001) and cannot be told apart from compulsory asking (p = 0.621) or from Full (p = 0.458). Kimi K2.6 resolved 72.80% with full information and 47.20% under compulsory asking, where it used the finish tool to ask its questions. It reached 61.60% with UA-Single and 69.40% with UA-Multi, still below Full (p < 0.05).

The counts show what the gate does. On the 500 tasks, Claude’s UA-Multi asked on 344 (68.8%), 1,053 questions in all or 3.06 per task where it asked; 41.8% came early in the trajectory, 43.4% in the middle and 14.8% late. UA-Single asked on 369 tasks (73.8%), 1.84 questions each, and compulsory asking on 496, with 97.6% of its questions early. Kimi’s UA-Multi asked on 435 tasks (87.0%), 3,791 questions in all or 8.71 per asked task, and its UA-Single on only 167 (33.4%).

To curb Kimi, the authors extended the intent agent’s prompt to hold back “... or when the user cannot provide any further details or explicitly asks you to keep working on the task”, which cut Kimi’s questions per task and still left most tasks flagged.

The best evidence that the gate asks where it matters is conditional. On the 344 tasks where Claude’s UA-Multi asked, it resolved 65.99%, against 44.48% for Hidden on the same tasks; on the 156 where it did not ask, 76.92% against 77.56%. Its ask rate rose with difficulty, from 62.37% for tasks under 15 minutes to 71.65% for 15 minutes to an hour and 78.57% for 1 to 4 hours. Kimi’s UA-Multi asked on 82.99% to 100% at every difficulty. The cost per task rose from $1.63 (Full) to $2.03 (UA-Single) and $3.50 (UA-Multi) for Claude, and from $0.63 to $1.00 for Kimi.

The authors write that the scaffold “closed the performance gap” with Full and gives well-calibrated asking, and read Kimi’s higher rate as a need for “targeted training beyond architectural design alone”. Their stated limits are simulated users, prompting with no training, frontier models only, and about twice the cost. The interactive conditions contain no fully specified task, so false asks are never measured, and “well calibrated” rests on the resolve rates where the gate did not ask. The gate decides whether to ask and has nothing to say about what, or about a user’s rule. The sentence added for Kimi is in effect a user’s instruction to the gate, and it did not bind. There is one run per setting, and the 1.0-point gap between compulsory asking and UA-Multi is within noise.

My reading is that the gate inherits its backbone’s calibration and adds none of its own.

CONTRA, a preprint, is about what to ask. Its problem is the second variant almost word for word. Agents silently resolve underspecified requirements and produce code that “appears correct but implements behavior the user never intended”, while asking about everything interrupts developers. It evaluates on ClarifyCodeBench, a separate benchmark of 419 underspecified and 80 fully specified function-level tasks, each with gold questions and answers. A method may ask at most five questions before writing its final program, and a GPT-4o judge matches the questions asked against the gold ones by a majority of three judgments.

The method runs in three stages, with one LLM doing every step within a given comparison. The input is the requirement as the user wrote it.

Discovery collects candidate questions. Each round generates questions twice, once looking at the requirement and the candidates found so far, and once from the requirement alone, and then merges duplicates. Rounds continue until two in a row add nothing new. Breadth is the aim here, since a question missed at this stage can never be asked. Each surviving question then gets two plausible answers, written out as two complete versions of the requirement that agree on everything except that question. Keeping the two versions identical elsewhere means that any later difference in behavior can be traced to the question and to nothing else.

Qualification filters the candidates with three checks. The first is relevance, meaning the question concerns how the program should behave and its two answers specify different behavior. The second is that the question is unresolved, meaning the requirement’s own text does not already settle it; a claim that the text settles it has to cite that text. The third is that the answer must change what the program does.

The behavioral check is executable, and it is what sets CONTRA apart. For each of the two versions of the requirement, the model writes three programs, and it writes at least seven inputs that are valid under both versions. All six programs run on those inputs. An output counts as stable when at least two of a version’s three programs agree on it, which keeps a single buggy program from creating a difference the question did not cause. The question passes if the two versions give different stable outputs on at least one input, that is, if the answer to the question would change what the code does. The authors add that “a failed check does not establish behavioral equivalence.”

Selection runs during the conversation. An answer from the user can settle another qualified question or make it unnecessary, so before each question the selector rereads the dialogue so far and either picks a remaining question that still needs the user, or stops. It also stops when no questions remain or five have been asked. A cheaper variant, Contra-Flash, qualifies questions only on demand and cuts qualification calls by 65.7% to 96.0%.

The scoring is two-sided by construction. Precision counts the fully specified tasks in its denominator, so any question there is a false positive. The paper also reports recall, F1 and a false-positive rate, the share of fully specified tasks with at least one question. A fourth score, the turn-discounted key-question rate (TKQR), gives more credit the earlier in the dialogue a gold question is found, so that the order of the questions counts as well as their set. The models were GPT-5-mini, GPT-5.5, Grok 4.5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro at low reasoning effort.

Averaged over the four, F1, precision and recall were 25.30, 41.61 and 19.28 for direct prompting, and 27.32, 33.02 and 23.60 for the Ask-or-Assume gate. ClarifyGPT, which asks where sampled programs disagree, scored 20.37, 16.59 and 28.29. CONTRA scored 41.20, 45.42 and 38.01, with a TKQR of 38.32. CONTRA leads on every model. It took direct prompting’s F1 from 16.86 to 35.36 for GPT-5-mini, 23.12 to 36.54 for Grok 4.5, 37.12 to 50.49 for Gemini 3.1 Pro and 24.10 to 42.39 for GPT-5.5. Pass@1, the share of tasks solved on the first try, barely moved on underspecified tasks, from 56.10 to 57.32 for Gemini 3.1 Pro and from 62.50 to 64.63 for GPT-5.5.

The ablations on GPT-5-mini put selection first. Removing adaptive selection drops F1 from 35.36 to 26.69, removing the resolution check to 29.13, the behavioral check to 32.58 and the relevance check to 32.54. With Qwen3.8-27B fixed, CONTRA reached an F1 of 19.86 against 9.63 inside Claude Code and 10.61 inside OpenHands, with a recall of 20.73% against 6.26% and 8.42%, while matching Claude Code’s false-positive rate of 5.00%.

The authors leave developer effort over long sessions unmeasured, have executable checks only for Python with pytest, and have not put the benchmark data in their repository. The tasks are single functions, so decisions inside a multi-file trajectory are not covered. The false-positive rate rests on 80 tasks, so 5.00% is 4 tasks and 32.50% is 26, on one model and one run. The behavioral check needs runnable code, and it cannot see decisions that leave outputs unchanged, such as style, choice of library or structure, which are many of a user’s “never ask about Y” cases.

Requiring a question to change executed behavior is the best answer I found to what makes a question worth asking, for the decisions that show up in behavior.

ClarEval, another preprint, asks whether the first question is the whole story.

It rewrites 750 coding tasks, 150 from HumanEval and 600 from LiveCodeBench, in three ways each with GPT-4o (a missing goal, missing premises, or ambiguous terminology), for 2,250 instances. Three senior engineers reviewed them in two stages with a Fleiss kappa of 0.82, discarding or rewriting about 12% of the raw items. On 50 items, the rewrites’ naturalness scored 4.12 against 4.25 for real StackOverflow questions (p > 0.05). Agents are tested in a single turn and over up to five turns.

Its simulated user is a script. Each task has pairs of trigger and response drawn from the ground truth (if the agent asks about order, the reply is “Sort it descending”), matched by keyword or phrase. Questions that match nothing get “I don’t have specific requirements for that; please follow standard best practices.” On 200 turns from GPT-5 Coder and Claude-Opus 4.1 logs, judged by GPT-4o, the script answered correctly 96.5% of the time, failing on metaphorical phrasing. A script cannot leak what it was not written to say.

Eleven agents and models were tested at temperature 0.1 with a cap of 512 tokens, from Aider and Codex built on GPT-5 to CodeLlama-70B and Qwen3-Coder. Key-question coverage (KQC) is the share of a task’s key intents that the questions cover. It ranged from 0.774 (O4) to 0.867 (GPT5-Coder) in a single turn, and from 0.376 (Aider-GPT5) to 0.754 (Claude-Opus 4.1) over several turns. The average turn at which a premise was resolved ranged from 1.493 (GPT5-Coder) to 2.396 (Qwen2.5-Coder).

More turns reshuffle the ranking. Single-turn and multi-turn performance correlated at only r = 0.32 (p < 0.05). Aider-GPT5 fell from 0.833 coverage in a single turn, sixth of eleven, to 0.376 over several turns, last. Clarification mattered for the code, with GPT-4o passing 8.94% of ambiguous tasks and 89.02% after clarification.

The authors conclude that detection is “largely solved” for top models, that efficiency is the issue, and that terminology is the main bottleneck. Without a clear-prompt control, an agent that asks about everything scores well, so the first claim cannot be checked, and over-asking is not measured. Several numbers disagree between text and tables,[31] one harness wraps models of very different kinds, which may explain Aider’s fall, and every question comes before any code is written. I would not cite its rankings. It does show that a benchmark of the first turn understates the problem.

When a question comes matters as much as whether it comes. Ask Early, Ask Late, Ask Right varied when in the trajectory a goal clarification arrives. Pass@3, success within three attempts, was 0.78 when it came at 10% of the trajectory and 0.39 by 70%. A late question may be worse than none, and for the guarantee a late answer means the work that depends on it has to be redone.

Gates and question selectors without training point the same way as CONTRA’s flat pass@1, in that better questions do not by themselves buy better outcomes. Uncertainty Decomposition for Clarification Seeking separates, by prompting alone, a model’s confidence in its action from its uncertainty about the request. It runs on versions of WebShop and ALFWorld where half the tasks are underspecified, with five backbones, and its gate compares the model’s own uncertainty with a fixed 0.5. It reports a clarification F1 “73% over ReAct+UE” on ALFWorld only, which reads as relative, given an absolute gap of about 0.28 in the body. Mean task success was 27.0%, against 28.6% for that baseline.

SAGE-Agent chooses questions by their expected value of information and reports 7% to 39% more coverage with 1.5 to 2.7 times fewer questions than prompting baselines, with its dial set in code. Ask-before-Plan separates clarification, execution and planning into agents that predict when clarification is needed from the conversation and the environment’s feedback, an early form of the intent agent. ClarifyGPT, already a baseline in CONTRA, scored below direct prompting, so disagreement between sampled programs picks many unimportant questions.

Newer agent benchmarks build the same hidden-information setup in other settings. ICAE-Bench builds projects from a fuzzy requirement, with a user agent that reveals hidden constraints. SWE-DIVE turns SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench into multi-turn tasks with personas such as a “Vague Requester”, and Asuka-Bench gives underspecified web tasks with feedback from tests. None gives per-model numbers on its abstract page, and like ClarEval none reports an ask rate on fully specified tasks, so their over-asking is unknown. RegretBench scores clarification as policy behavior against a reference policy, by intent resolution, interaction cost, ineffective clarification and regret, in question answering and product recommendation. It finds that “models with similar accuracy can differ substantially in efficiency”, in how they handle different user behaviors and in when they stop. Its regret framing has the right shape for scoring asking in both directions.

What training buys

Training is parked as a method here, so trained askers are the comparison point, showing what a model can learn about asking when asking is the objective. CollabLLM shows the typical trade. Its premise is that RLHF rewards the next turn, so models answer an ambiguous request passively. Its fix is a multiturn-aware reward, which scores a response by what it does to the rest of the conversation, where the usual reward judges it as the next turn alone.

The reward is computed in three steps. The input is the conversation so far and one candidate response. First, a simulated user continues the conversation from that response for a window of 2 turns, and this is done 3 times to get several possible futures. Second, each future conversation is scored: the task metric on the final solution against the reference, minus a cost for the tokens the user reads and writes (capped at 1), plus an interactivity score from 0 to 1 given by a Claude-3.5-Sonnet judge. Third, the scores are averaged, and the average is the candidate’s reward. A clarifying question that costs a turn now but leads to a correct solution later scores well under this reward, where a reward for the next turn alone would favor a confident answer.

The simulated user is GPT-4o-mini with the target problem, told to make vague demands early, make occasional mistakes, state preferences and stay goal-oriented. It was not formally validated, and the authors note that open-source simulators “get confused” and act as assistants. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct was trained with LoRA, small low-rank updates to the weights, on 500 simulated conversations for each of three tasks: editing Medium articles (scored by BLEU), coding problems from BigCodeBench (pass rate) and level-5 MATH problems (judged accuracy). The reward decides what it learns from. For supervised fine-tuning, the top-ranked response at each turn is kept to extend the conversation. For DPO, the responses at each turn are ranked and split into chosen and rejected pairs. The online variants, PPO and online DPO, go on computing the reward for the model’s own responses as it trains.

On BigCodeBench the pass rate went from 9.3 for the base to 11.0 when prompted to be proactive, 13.0 with online DPO and 14.0 with PPO, while interactivity went from 22.0 to 33.7 prompted and 52.0 trained. Document editing went from a BLEU of 32.2 to 38.5, with interactivity from 46.0 to 62.0 prompted and 92.0 trained. Maths accuracy went from 11.0 to 16.5 while conversations fell from 3.40k to 2.37k tokens. Against the best baseline, the headline gains are 18.5% on the task, 13.3% on efficiency and 46.3% on interactivity.

In a study with 201 MTurk workers, documents scored 8.50 of 10, 63.8% found the trained model highly engaging against 42.6% for the base, and they spent 10.4% less time, though some complained that it asked again for information already given. In the ablations, larger windows helped and immediate rewards fell short. No variance is reported, and the coding gains are small in absolute terms. Its own list of limits names the GPT-4o-mini simulator, the cost of forward sampling ($0.00174 to $0.00685 per sample, for windows of 1 and 3), users who sometimes found the model bland, and unnecessary questions that remain.

The result that matters for this problem is a side effect. On Abg-CoQA, conversational questions with ambiguous and clear inputs, tested zero-shot, the trained model picked the right action on 52.84% of ambiguous inputs, against 16.26% for its base and 15.44% for GPT-4o. On clear inputs it picked the right action on 72.32%, against 90.40% for its base and 95.60% for GPT-4o. Its macro F1 of 55.08% sits between the base’s 53.31% and GPT-4o’s 56.62%. It learned to ask more, which is a different skill from knowing when to ask, and the rate is fixed in training, out of the user’s reach.

The pattern recurs across trained askers. STaR-GATE teaches Mistral-7B to ask by keeping simulated conversations that raise the probability of a gold response. Its responses beat the initial model’s on 72% of tasks, and without regularization the model “may forget how to respond and instead always ask questions”. Modeling Future Conversation Turns ranks asking against answering by simulated future outcomes, fine-tuning Llama2-7B, Gemma-7B and Llama3-8B. Its best model by F1 asks on every query, while its selective model averages 1.56 turns and its F1 drops to 24.3 (Llama2), so always asking maximizes F1 and asking selectively costs accuracy.

ACT trains Zephyr 7B on contrastive pairs that clarify or answer, and reaches 43.6 execution match on AmbigSQL against 21.9 for supervised fine-tuning with 50 conversations, though only 52.3 against 51.0 with 250. ClarifyCoder fine-tunes DeepSeek Coder 7B on 29,896 samples of APPS problems made ambiguous, incomplete or inconsistent. It raises the communication rate on HumanEvalComm to 63.61% from 24.12% while keeping HumanEval pass@1 (74.85% against 73.62%). Neither ACT nor ClarifyCoder measured over-asking on clear problems.

A few trained askers do not drift. APA fine-tunes on 1,382 to 3,216 examples that the model labels itself, calling a query ambiguous when its entropy passes a threshold. It improves F1 on unambiguous queries as well (AmbigWebQuestions 24.87 to 49.15), with the lowest rate of misaligned clarification among its baselines. A Small Model Asks Better trains a LoRA classifier for whether to clarify, with hard negatives, and reaches 96.6% accuracy on about 3,900 examples; of these papers it is the one designed against over-asking.

AskToAct removes key parameters from tool-call queries and builds dialogues that ask at the point each parameter is needed. Qwen2.5-7B recovers intent in 63.88% of cases with full fine-tuning and 57.68% with LoRA. With LoRA it asks again for information already given in 6.80% of cases, down from 12.43%. It is the only data process found that places a question where the missing item is used, though inside a single tool call.

For agents, the best small result is an information-gain clarifier on tau-bench. A separate Qwen3-1.7B clarifier was trained with reinforcement learning on a reward for information gain, from 500 tau-retail trajectories cut into 2,676 decisions. It cut clarifier calls per task from 4.2 to 1.3 and raised success across five agent backbones from 23.6% to 27.3%, where untrained general models over-clarify at 3.9 to 5.1 turns per task. With a cooperative simulated user the policy collapsed into vague questions. A strict one that replied “Just do what I asked.” fixed it, which is a lesson for any simulated user.

CLARITI trains an 8B question module for underspecified issues and matches GPT-5’s resolution rate with 41% fewer questions. SWEET-RL gives credit for each turn through a critic that sees the hidden reference code. On ColBench’s collaborative backend tasks, Llama-3.1-8B reaches 40.4% success, equal to GPT-4o, against 22.4% zero-shot and 34.4% with multi-turn DPO, though it does not count questions.

The rest confirm that asking is learnable with modest data. ALFA trains medical questions attribute by attribute, and an 8B model reaches 88.08% diagnostic accuracy against 72.52%. CIGAsk rewards counterfactual information gain, with a bonus for ambiguity. Teaching LMs to Gather Information Proactively rewards questions that surface new information and reports, in its abstract, 18% over o3-mini on automatic metrics.[32]

Apart from PPP and the steerable policies above, none of these conditions on a user’s rule; each fixes how much to ask during training, as a constant the user cannot move, and several drift toward asking more.

After the question

Asking has a cost beyond the interruption, and Is That Clear?, a preprint, measured it with people.

Clarifying questions are usually judged by what they elicit and by how satisfied users are. This study asks whether they also change how much people scrutinize, and rely on, the answer that follows, including a wrong one, and whether that differs between the parts of a decision the questions covered and the parts they did not.

There were 180 participants from Prolific (188 completed, and 8 failed attention checks), 90 per condition between subjects, paid £3 plus £0.20 for careful answers, a bonus that did not depend on finding errors. Each saw three of six everyday decisions: earbuds, a smart lock, a trip to New York, an apartment, a car and a group dinner. Each decision has three sub-decisions. In one condition the response is shown directly. In the other, two clarifying questions come first, one for each of two sub-decisions, each offering two options framed with a benefit and a downside.

The questions were generated with GPT-5, and the responses were written in advance. There are 72 paragraphs crossing each preference option with satisfying or violating a hard constraint, and every violating paragraph carries exactly one violation, checked by two researchers. The violations are of constraints, because in a 21-person pilot almost no one caught factual errors. Eighteen counterbalanced schedules place each violation once next to a covered sub-decision and once next to an uncovered one. The analysis uses mixed-effects models with intercepts for participant and scenario, and Holm correction for multiple tests.

Questions made people check less. With questions, people reviewed faster (55.0 against 80.7 seconds, p = .003) and evaluated faster (60.4 against 81.0 seconds, p = .024). They accepted 52% of violating responses unchanged, against 39% without questions (p = .034), and 60% of all responses against 46% (p = .024), with correct responses accepted 76% against 61%. Confidence was higher (68.1 against 61.1, p = .032), and so was satisfaction (5.3 against 4.9 of 7, p = .043). Perceived alignment was higher (5.4 against 4.9, p = .020) and the felt need to double-check lower (5.1 against 5.7, p = .009), while trust and usefulness did not differ. People with questions also wrote a follow-up request on 40% of trials, against 54% without.

Within the question condition, a violation in a covered sub-decision was accepted 60% of the time against 43% for an uncovered one (p = .019), and reported 23% against 37% (p = .019). Across conditions, violations were reported 30% of the time with questions and 38% without, and noticed 40% against 50% (p = .075), neither significant. Of the violations people said they noticed, 24% were still accepted; of those they did not notice, 92%.

The authors propose grounding and personalization as the mechanisms, and draw three design implications: show the scope of what was resolved, verify elicited content on the system’s side, and prompt users to check constraints. Their stated limits are small cells, low-stakes everyday tasks, a fixed two questions, pre-generated responses, and non-significant results that may reflect power, and no preregistration is mentioned in the parts I read. The firm effect is on acceptance, since the drop in reporting across conditions is not significant, and the 60% against 43% compares covered and uncovered sub-decisions within one condition. The tasks are consumer decisions with short responses, a long way from code review.

For the first variant, the study means “ask every time” has a hidden cost, since people check the step that follows an answered question less. For the guarantee, it means the agent has to check that an answer was applied everywhere it matters, because the user checks less once they have answered.

Getting an answer is also different from using it. Clarification Is Not Enough fine-tuned the decision to clarify or answer and found, by its abstract, that on PACIFIC “final answer accuracy remains substantially lower even when the model takes the correct action”. SWE-Touch tests what happens when the ground shifts under the agent. A simulated user edits code relevant to the task mid-run and sends a message when the agent reaches it, and across nine models the resolve rate on SWE-bench Verified falls by 7.7 points, as agents keep conflicting code or replace it “without sufficiently re-inspecting the repository”. It changes code and not instructions, and it does not report whether agents ask about the edit, but it is the nearest measurement found of the guarantee failing.

SWE-Together takes 109 repository-level tasks from 11,260 real developer sessions in which users clarify, add constraints and correct mistakes. It replays the intent with a reactive simulator and scores final correctness and the number of corrective turns. By its abstract, “stronger agents generally achieve higher final success rates while requiring fewer interventions”. It counts corrective turns, and whether dependent work was redone is not scored.

Disclosure, the other move the second variant allows, has one direct measurement. CONTRA also ships as a plugin for Claude Code. A hook records the working tree when a request is submitted, and when the agent finishes, the plugin compares the tree with that snapshot. It extracts the decisions stated in the agent’s final report and, in parallel, has claude-opus-5 review the requirement, the code changes and the repository without reading that report. It merges the two lists, and for an unresolved implementation choice it writes a test that tells the agent’s choice from an alternative and runs it in isolated copies.

The evaluation used 40 single-function tasks from commits in Rich, Flask, Requests, Click, HTTPX and Typer, 20 underspecified and 20 fully specified, with Claude Code 2.1.261 running claude-opus-5. The labels were made by claude-opus-5 and reviewed by hand. Of the 262 decisions found, 116 (44.3%) appeared only in the independent review, absent from the agent’s own report.[33] With the plugin, the share of report entries naming the unspecified information rose from 4.3% to 59.7%, those naming an alternative from 7.1% to 47.8%, and those naming the information, the choice and an alternative together from 2.9% to 46.3%. A demonstration run cost $0.70 and took about 130 seconds.

It is one agent, 40 tasks, one run, and labels from the same family of models as the agent, and the plugin reports after the fact without holding up a step until a decision is disclosed.

Still, in this one study close to half the decisions an agent made were missing from what it told the user.

Two papers make assumptions explicit by other routes. PROPER Agents has the model list a task’s explicit and implicit dimensions and then answer with them, neither asking nor extrapolating, and reports “up to 84% gains in single-turn evaluation”. Its dimensions are close to an inventory of decisions, with evidence at the level of an abstract. Verbalizing LLMs’ assumptions prompts models to state what they assume about the user, then probes and steers along those assumptions. “Seeking validation” is the most frequent pair of words among them, and steering reduces social sycophancy. These are assumptions about the user, a different object from the task decisions an agent should show the user.

On the theory side, When to Ask a Question models the trade between the user’s burden and how well the user’s preferences are represented, with no data, and finds that correlated preferences favor mixing inference with asking.

What is settled, and what is open

Read together, these papers support the opening claim and three more: prompted asking moves globally and in directions that depend on the model, training lifts adherence to whatever it trains and tends to over-ask, and with the model fixed the harness alone moves asking 3 to 6.5 times. Asking also costs more than the interruption, and a question asked at the right moment does not ensure the answer is used.

Three readings need narrowing. PPP’s headline comparison with GPT-5 suggests that prompts do not bind, and that needs a model and a setting. PPP’s untrained open base, prompted GPT-4.1 on research and Claude Sonnet 4 under a strong prompt each did well in one setting, and Gemini 3 Flash steered in the right direction. What holds is that prompting does not bind reliably across models. PPP also does not show that training binds “never ask”, because its metric drops the episodes with no questions, and ClarEval’s “detection is largely solved” cannot be checked without a clear-prompt control.

The gaps, as far as a limited search can tell, line up with the frame. A rule scoped to a topic, such as “ask about X, never about Y”, is tested nowhere; PPP’s only_begin, first_try and do_selection come closest, and they govern when and how, never what. Training data where a user’s topical rule governs asking does not exist either, and none places questions at the decision point inside a long coding run. A rule about asking that changes in the middle of a session has never been scored for whether behavior flips with it, though the steerability metric is the template, on question answering with a number.

Labels for each decision along a trajectory, as supplied by the user, elicited by a question or assumed, are also missing; PPP’s effort labels and CONTRA’s behavioral check label questions, never decisions. Disclosure is measured once, after the fact, and not as a rate during the trajectory. The guarantee, that an answer or a late change reaches the work that depends on it, is measured only indirectly, by SWE-Touch, SWE-Together and Clarification Is Not Enough. A simulated user was validated against people in one paper of the ten, TellOnce, and variance is reported in four: the steerable policies, Ask or Assume?, TellOnce and Is That Clear?.

A test of both variants can take most of its parts from these papers. Ambig-SWE gives paired full and hidden tasks scored in both directions, PPP gives effort labels to judge whether a question was needed, and the steerable policies give the flip of a rule with the task held fixed. CONTRA gives a behavioral check for which decisions are consequential, ClarEval a scripted user whose fallback reply works as a license to decide, TellOnce compiled checks where unknown is never compliance, and Is That Clear? the case for checking each answer against the work. None of them yet scores the second variant whole, on one trajectory, by asking whether every decision the human left open ended in a question, a disclosure, or a guess the human never heard about.
