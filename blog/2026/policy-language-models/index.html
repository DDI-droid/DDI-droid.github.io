Policy Language Models

Created on June 30, 2026

2026   ·   llm   meta-reasoning   ·   research

This post introduces what I’m working on right now, Policy Language Models. The paper is linked at the end.

Updated August 2026. The first half is revised in place; from “The invariant” onward is new, and it reports what happened when I tried to prove the part of this argument that turned on the harness. Some of it did not survive. Alex Zhang’s work over the summer is the reason, and the RLM section now characterizes that work correctly rather than the way I first read it.

The trajectory

Frontier large language models and the harnesses built around them can now tackle complex long-horizon problems, resolve software issues, conduct deep research, and drive hours-long sessions. The gains that enabled this have largely arrived through inference-time scaling. Models are trained to spend more tokens deliberating, and ReAct systems extend those capabilities by interleaving reasoning with actions and observations. ReAct remains the dominant substrate for this work, and a ReAct trajectory is a growing sequence of those thoughts, actions, and observations.

The trajectory only grows, every thought, action, and observation is appended, and LLM outputs degrade as the context window fills. The most popular general remedy is context compaction, where the trajectory is repeatedly summarized when it exceeds a length threshold. Compaction loses information, and it does not repair the reasoning inside the window. Trajectories pushed to length fail in characteristic ways, exploration that fragments the reasoning, self-reflection that strains against load-bearing mistakes, the capacity of the window itself; the short version is a single mismatch. The model repeatedly reaches for reasoning shapes the autoregressive substrate cannot hold: it branches to weigh alternatives, reverts to undo a wrong step, and explores before committing, yet every such move is flattened back into one forward sequence. I think these failures act as a dampener on reasoning LLMs, and that solving them will yield far more capable reasoners.

The failures are not only about length. A ReAct loop bundles two separable things, the capability of interleaving thought with code, and the protocol that ends the call: a disclosed round budget, an obligation to answer, a return channel, pressure at the end. Frameworks ship them fused, because a call that cannot end is useless to a caller who only receives a value. In my own runs the protocol failed on its own terms, sub-agents surrendering early and returning nothing, returning a progress report where an answer belonged, returning the literal string done. These are artifacts of the channel, not of the deduction, and they appear wherever the channel is present.

Taking work off the line

Recently, Zhang, Kraska, and Khattab introduced Recursive Language Models (RLMs), a general-purpose inference paradigm for dramatically scaling the effective input and output lengths of LLMs. Their key insight is that an arbitrarily long prompt should not be fed into the neural network directly, but treated as part of the environment that the LLM symbolically and recursively interacts with. Given a prompt \(P\), an RLM initializes a REPL environment in which \(P\) is set as the value of a variable, and the model writes code that peeks into and decomposes \(P\), observes the side effects of execution, and invokes the LLM itself on as many slices of the input as necessary.

I first read this as a result about input processing, and said so in the earlier version of this post. That was a misreading, and Zhang’s work over the summer makes the actual claim explicit. The claim is about composition. A harness induces an equivalence relation over tasks: two problems that share latent structure, however different their vocabulary and their length, reduce to nearly the same token trajectory in the root, because the harness offloads the domain-specific material into sub-calls and exposes only the structure needed to choose the next operation. A good harness shapes each call so that every observation is, in his phrase, locally in-distribution. What he demonstrates is that training on short tasks generalizes to tasks eight to thirty-two times longer, and that training on one domain transfers to others. Input length is where this is measured. It is not what is being claimed.

This matters here because it is the strongest available statement of what a harness is for, and it is not the statement I was making. His invariant is a property of the model’s input distribution, that every call the model sees looks like something it was trained on. Mine is a property of the trajectory’s information state. Those are different objects, and most of this post is about the second one. He hedges the reach of his own conjecture, that the tasks people care about can “apparently almost always” be decomposed naturally, and he offers no characterization of the training set that would suffice; I read the hedges as honest rather than as an opening.

Policy Language Models

We introduce Policy Language Models (PLMs), a general-purpose meta-reasoning inference paradigm for dramatically scaling the reasoning power of LLMs. The key insight is that reasoning to generate computations, computations that do tasks or find answers or reveal information, gives rise to meta-reasoning. This behavior sheds the failures plaguing ReAct while keeping all its benefits, offers additional benefits, and, I believe, carries a far higher ceiling for reasoning LLMs. We define meta-reasoning as the behavior of identifying problems, reasoning out the computation that solves them, and assimilating its work. A PLM is made of two parts: the harness, where computations are authored and run as policies, and the model, trained to reason them out.

Assimilation is the word that carries the most weight there, and it is the part of this program that survived everything below. A PLM does not merely dispatch work, it takes the work back, and the trajectory that authored a policy is also the trajectory that reads what the policy produced, decides what it means, and reasons onward from it. The harness is built so that nothing produced anywhere in the computation is out of that trajectory’s reach.

What an RLM does for its input, a PLM does for all its computation. Instead of reasoning and computing inline, a PLM authors policies: programmatic artifacts it can run, edit, and compose, and lets them carry the work a trajectory would otherwise hold: the mechanical steps, the reasoning, the interaction with the world. Policies live and execute in a persistent REPL environment. Our harness also provides sub-LLM functional policies, so a PLM can create policies capable of reasoning and computation. The PLM can control what a policy returns: our harness’s constraint abstraction lets it fix the invariants of any policy’s output, the sub-LLM policies included. The REPL is the only way a PLM acts; it is, however, not confined to only authoring policies there; it can inspect, compute, and act directly, much as an RLM does. What makes it a meta-reasoner is not a prohibition on doing the work itself but a disposition, trained into it, to author policies that do the work better than it would inline.

The trajectory itself is responsible for abstract reasoning. There the PLM reasons over the goal and authors the policies that set its plans in motion: policies that test its hypotheses, that process its inputs, that write and emit the code it owes. A PLM’s job is to reason, and it engineers its instruments with an understanding of the goal, sees the work they did, and reasons onward. And since reading an input is itself computation, the RLM capability comes along as a special case, a long input can sit in the environment and be worked through with policies.

A PLM reasons about the goal abstractly and expresses the computation as policies. Here, the PLM authors a single evolutionary policy that breeds candidate policies through sub-LLM builders, selects among them by played outcomes, and folds only gated winners into its deliverable.

What the substrate buys is compounding. A policy authored once can be rerun, revised, and composed into larger ones, so the work of reasoning accumulates as artifacts rather than as tokens, and assimilation keeps the accumulation honest, every artifact’s product folded back into the reasoning that ordered it. The substrate alone, however, does not produce the behavior. In the observational runs I did before this program, current LLMs placed on the PLM harness did not spontaneously meta-reason, they ground the work inline out of habit, or scattered one-off delegations that were at best half-cooked. The disposition has to be trained in. That is what I take from it, and it is why the rest of this is about training.

The harness

A Policy Language Model is a large language model that meta-reasons: it reasons about the goal on its own trajectory and expresses the computation as policies in a persistent programming environment \(\mathcal{E}\). Each round the model thinks abstractly, then acts through one code tool call that executes in \(\mathcal{E}\) and updates its state. A PLM trajectory holds only three things: the abstract reasoning, the code, and the code’s output as the tool result.

It is more honest to say the harness has four axes than three primitives. What a program controls is the conversation a generation conditions on, how persistent context evolves between generations, the law the output is sampled from, and which model continues a given context; llm, react, and react_auto are points in that lattice, not the capabilities themselves. Said that way, the obvious objection answers itself: the axes are the base, the primitives are bundles of them.

Meta-reasoning is the axis of inference along which the model reasons about the goal and reasons about generating the computations that will help attain it. It holds together abstract reasoning about the goal with the design of compute structures for the tasks that goal breaks into, and what binds them is the cycle where the model decides what to do and how to do it, inlines the task or expresses it as computation, takes back what the computation produced, and reasons on.

A policy is any computation the PLM expresses rather than performs. The definition is behavioral, not syntactic, and the harness gives it support: the @policy decorator turns a function or a class into a named artifact that persists across rounds, holds global scope so it is used by name and never imported, and can be edited in place and duplicated. Authored once, it can be rerun, edited, and composed into larger policies.

The harness gives the PLM its LLM policies to build with. llm is a single call. react_auto is an LLM with the python tool, run in ReAct rounds under the termination protocol, which is to say a conventional sub-agent. react is the same loop with the protocol removed: it runs exactly the rounds it is given and stops, still open, and the name RETURN does not exist inside it. The split came out of the failures above. Termination becomes a decision the policy makes from outside, having read what the sub-computation actually did, rather than a decision the sub-model makes under deadline pressure, and react injects no text of its own, no budget, no round count, no briefing about its own mechanism, so every word in a stepped trajectory is either the author’s or the model’s.

These are the bare minimum on their own, and can be used to build arbitrarily complex information processing circuits. The building, however, is deliberate work: every sub-llm call begins empty. Every input, helper, prompt, any policy it will use, the @policy decorator and the LLM policies themselves, are handed to it. Nothing about a sub-llm is ambient, so a working one has to be constructed. Fanning out is never a reflex but a decision the PLM has to make and build, so it decomposes only when it decides to. Computation can never be fully controlled or predicted, but a harness decides who holds what control there is. Ours concentrates it in the PLM.

Constraints and weaving complete the harness. A constraint is a way to define invariants over a computation’s output, authored as a typed class, and the name undersells it: a constraint is compiled into the schema the call decodes under, so the model cannot emit a violating structure in the first place, which is a different operation from validating a returned string and asking again, which resamples the same unconstrained distribution. An LLM’s judgment so becomes a typed value in ordinary control flow: react_auto("...", constraint=Bool) yields a validated value, or None when the budget runs out without one, and policy code branches on the result, if-shaped rather than try-shaped, by design.

Weaving makes the trajectory and the namespace ordinary objects. Messages pass by reference, so a react run over a list leaves its turns on that list, and the list can be handed onward, edited, pruned, or restored, never as pasted text. The sub-agent’s namespace is a dictionary the caller owns, so everything the sub-computation defined, its variables and the functions it wrote, is readable and callable by the policy after the call and between rounds; granting is putting a key in that dictionary, and there is no session object anywhere, a session is the pair of containers the author is holding. Branching the namespace took a measurement to trust: deepcopy treats functions as atomic, so a copied branch’s helpers stay bound to the parent’s globals, and a branch we watched read the parent’s data and drove the parent’s counter while its own stayed at zero. The harness therefore ships copy_namespace, which rebuilds a sub-agent’s functions over the child dictionary.

The invariant

Here is the thing this program is actually about, stated as two conditions rather than one sentence.

D1, sufficiency. At any point of the root trajectory, nothing the trajectory has produced becomes unrecoverable, whether or not the reasoner knew at the time that it would matter. The root need not carry the information itself. It must carry either the information, or information about the information, enough to recover it on demand from the environment. Assimilation is what this condition licenses: work can leave the trajectory without leaving the reasoner’s reach.

D2, minimality. Subject to D1, the root trajectory carries as little as possible. Every token on it should be reasoning about the goal, not the material the reasoning was performed on.

Meta-reasoning is the disposition that preserves D1 and D2 together across the cycles of authoring, running, and assimilating, and the pair is the point. A long-context model has sufficiency without minimality, one window holding everything and losing whatever eviction takes. An aggressive offloader has minimality without sufficiency, a short root over sub-computations whose reasoning dies at the return. Zhang’s invariant, every observation locally in-distribution, says what the model must generalize over. It does not say what the root can still reach.

An RLM cannot express D1, and the check is the contract, not a benchmark: rlm_query returns a string, and no channel returns the turns. A sub-call’s trajectory is invisible to the root, which holds only what the sub-model chose to say, where a PLM policy holds the trajectory itself, every turn the sub-model wrote, its code and its answers. Hidden reasoning tokens cross the wire for neither system, my own harness strips that field, so the comparison is over what actually crosses. Zhang notes that his roots keep printing task-specific information back into the main context, and calls it undesirable for generalization; I read it as a system straining against exactly this missing channel.

Role authorship is the other half of the contract argument. Under the string contract the mapping from provenance to role is fixed by protocol, code output enters as tool or user material and an assistant turn can only be genuinely generated, while a PLM policy places any content under any role: a commitment the model appears to have made, an observation the environment appears to have produced, a system update arriving mid-history.

What I set out to show, and what happened

The theory above has a companion claim that seemed obvious in June, and it is the claim I spent the summer trying to establish. If a PLM can hold objects an RLM cannot, the space of policies it can author should be strictly larger in a way that shows up as capability. Write \(\Pi_X\) for the interaction structures a program can control under contract \(X\), and \(R(\pi)\) for the tasks a policy \(\pi\) gets right; a trained PLM covers the union of \(R(\pi)\) over the policies \(\theta\) can write. The bet was that this union outruns what any decomposition-shaped system reaches. That would have been a direct challenge to Zhang’s conjecture.

I built three harnesses to test it. One is the full PLM harness. One is the same harness with exactly the beyond-string-contract surface removed, so a policy there is arbitrary code around string-in, string-out calls: the agentic region, and, with the round budget fixed, the system message withheld, and the namespace switch off, the RLM region. The third is a further restricted variant for a separate experiment. A test asserts the diff between them, so the boundary is materialized rather than promised.

The comparison did not go my way. On an interactive deduction task the best agentic policy scored 0.70 against the best PLM policy’s 0.60 over ten development instances, one instance apart, and pooled over every run the ordering holds at 0.80 against 0.53, with run-to-run variance large enough that the PLM arm scored five of six and then two of five on overlapping instances. One more number empties both figures of meaning as a barrier test: an offline pass over the ten knowledge books, done before any policy ran, put the task’s own ceiling at 0.616 under its action budget, because the books over-claim and the observations rule out the gold artifact in seven instances of ten. Both arms were pressed against noise in the data, not against either contract.

The emulation was harder to argue with. I flattened the PLM policy into its best string-contract form, everything it did but re-fed as one labeled transcript, and it reproduced the PLM maximum exactly on the iteration set, beat it on the full ten, and did it at sixty-four percent of that arm’s token cost. Instrumenting both arms said why. Graded against a reference table, re-feeding read the books at ninety-one percent exact-rule fidelity, precisely what the live agentic session read them at, while the best PLM arm sat at 77.2, and thirty-six of its thirty-eight lost rules came from a single instance where fidelity fell to 5.3 percent, a sixteen-round session in which the model simply stopped populating the namespace dict. A session pathology beat the PLM arm, not a reasoning deficit. Against my own case I will also record that those PLM arms never passed constraint and never made a protocol-bearing call, reading their answers out of the namespace after plain react rounds, so every loss on that side was authoring rather than a mechanism the contract had withheld.

Three constructed tasks stacked the mechanisms PLM has and the string contract does not, and none produced a gap I could defend. The first swung two thirds the wrong way on the small model and converged at ceiling on the larger one, where every arm finished perfect and the task stopped discriminating. The second scored zero everywhere, partly through difficulty and partly through a PLM-side degeneracy worth naming, one instance spending fourteen rounds politely refusing to call its own tool and asking me to run it and paste the result back. The third sat at chance in all four arms, and one of the four was RLM-shaped by construction, single-shot string calls over a context held as a REPL variable with no round budget exposed to the sub-call, so the comparison was never only against my own agentic region.

Two findings arrived unbidden, and both cut against the theory. A fresh context per phase acts as an error reset, deleting the degeneracy a long session accumulates, so at small scale the boundaries I had counted as losses compounded a cleansing; on the larger model the degeneracy is gone, the reset has nothing left to cure, and the sign moved without ever flipping my way. And curation is a liability when the distillation is noisy: the policy that pruned stale reports and told the model its state table was its memory scored zero, while the same composition with the raw reports left in the weave scored a third. The redundancy I removed was the redundancy the model repaired itself with.

One finding runs the other way, and it is about the protocol rather than the contract. On the qualifying model the naive bubble scored zero, piping its first observation straight into RETURN and quitting on all six instances, and the naive chopped arm scored 0.50, its bubbles satisfying the return obligation without performing the commit the task actually graded; ordinary code around the call repaired both to a clean sweep. The protocol tax is real, it is the only irreducible I saw bite at that scale, and it is the one most cheaply patched from outside. That is why the emulation ties.

The mechanism behind the null constrains everyone, not only me. Code computes whatever its inputs determine, which is why a solver inside a policy beats any amount of deliberation about the same clauses; but where a task’s difficulty is judgment rather than computation, the model is the only source, and a policy’s output distribution is a computable transformation of the conditionals it can elicit. A policy can select among samples, condition them differently, compose them, amplify them with a checker. It cannot create probability the model does not assign. At vanishing probability under every reachable conditioning, no shape helps; at decent probability, cheap shapes already win.

One thing escapes that argument, and it is a theorem rather than a hope. Give a policy a sound polynomial-time checker and \(k\) samples, and its success on an obligation is one minus the chance of \(k\) failures, so the region it handles reliably strictly contains the model’s own and tends to the model’s support as \(k\) grows. A harness moves a task to where the model is reliable; a verifying policy only needs it possible. Verification is also the top rung of a ladder, voting, search, invariant regulation, cross-examination, constrained decoding beneath it, each demanding a weaker symbolic object, and a policy is the closure of those generators under composition, not the list. Every rung has an output-side form that any coded harness owns, about which I claim nothing, and a trajectory-side form that only a participant in the computation owns. My probes exercised the output side almost exclusively, which is consistent with the nulls, and is exactly where the theory says a gap should not be.

So, exactly: what fell is the prediction, that the larger authorable space shows up as an accuracy gap on tasks I could build and afford. The containment stands, an RLM decomposition is a PLM policy at the one level of nesting both systems actually run, call depth being fixed by my kernel as an experimental condition while orchestration depth stays unbounded on both sides. And the invariant stands. D1 remains inexpressible under the string contract for the contract’s own reason, and assimilation, which D1 licenses, is the part of this idea I still believe in.

What has to be trained

Placed on the harness untrained, current models delegate but do not meta-reason, and the affinity improves little under prompting, even with detailed, optimized system prompts, reasoning models hold to the habits of the single trajectory. The disposition has to be trained in, and the training is not only subtractive. Taking work off the trajectory is half of it, the other half is new duties, authoring, granting, briefing, weaving, that no model has ever been trained to perform.

The original plan was to train a model that authors policies in a space whose coverage exceeds the decomposition space, and the summer removed the first half of that plan. What remains, and what I think is the more interesting target anyway, is training for the invariant: a model rewarded for keeping D1 and D2 together, which is to say rewarded for what it assimilates and for what it declines to carry. How that is enforced, how it is rewarded, and how it is discovered at all remain open, and I leave them open deliberately.

There is one direction I have not tested and want to. Because the harness is a participant in its own computation rather than a caller of it, the supervision available inside it is different in kind from task-level outcome: which branch verified, where an invariant broke and at which round, which continuation survived. Those labels are visible to a system that owns the trajectory and structurally invisible to one that does not. If contracts determine what supervision exists, then the contract question is a training question, and that is the version of this program I would most like to be wrong about in the other direction.

Appendix: the experimental program

I am writing this before the program is finished, and the honest state is uneven, so here is what was planned, what has been run, and what has not.

E1, the gradient. Whether policy shape alone, holding the model and the task fixed, moves capability. This part came out clean. On the deduction task the same executor moved from 0.17 to 0.70 across policy designs, a factor of four from authoring alone, the endpoints coming from a six-instance baseline and a ten-instance final. Every number in this post is on the small model I develop against, not yet requalified at the scale I would use for a paper, so I report the gradient as a strong indication rather than a settled measurement. Zhang’s harness results and the Meta-Harness line of work point the same way from a different direction.

E2, the barrier. Whether some task exists where the best policy in the full space beats the best policy under the string contract by a margin that survives a serious attempt at emulation. This part failed, and the verdicts are better named than counted. The duel came in under its own threshold with the gap running the wrong way. The first constructed task had its kill condition fire at two thirds in the wrong direction, then converged at ceiling on the larger model. The second was voided by a floor effect. The third was discarded on audit, a row-level check finding the small model mis-recording between half and two thirds of its own observations, so its four-way tie was noise. A fourth run, a composed policy stacking six mechanisms at once, was discarded before reporting when I found the comparison arm had no parse-retry loop while the constrained arm effectively did, an authoring asymmetry rather than a contract one. And the minimal exhibit for role authorship hit its pre-registered threshold from the wrong side, roles intact at twenty-four of twenty-four against the flattened version’s twenty-two, a difference of 0.083 against a threshold of 0.10, so that axis is behaviorally inert on the shape I tested at the scale I tested it.

I want to be careful about how much all that means. It rules out the gaps I knew how to construct, on the tasks I could afford, at the scale I could afford. It does not rule out the regime I think the theory points at, window-pressured, long-horizon work where the eviction of one’s own past reasoning becomes irreversible, though I am saying that after the null rather than before it, and it is the first thing I would want an unsympathetic reader to press on. I did not get to that regime, it is expensive, and time ran out before the budget did. One axis never got loaded at all, selective granting, and the reason is a design fact rather than a schedule: an agentic policy controls its own globals, so it can quarantine by keeping the sensitive object in a local variable and passing a sanitized string, which closes the naive version of that task before it starts.

E3, authoring. Whether models can author into this space at all, and how that ability varies with what the harness exposes. Not run. This is also the question Alex’s work bears on most directly, since the compositional-generalization result is precisely about training a root to produce the right decompositions, and any serious version of E3 has to be read against it.

A caution about the method, which is the part of this I would defend regardless of the result. Two probes were discarded on audit rather than reported, one because the model was mis-recording most of its own observations and every arm was scoring at chance, and one because I had given the comparison arm a worse deal than the arm I wanted to win. Both would have been publishable-looking results. Neither was real, and the second was mine.
